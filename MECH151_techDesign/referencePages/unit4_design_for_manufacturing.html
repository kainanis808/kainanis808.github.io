Unit 4 — Weeks 14–16

Design for Manufacturing (DFM)

From model to prototype

This unit closes the loop from CAD model to physical part. That means preparing the files each process actually needs, understanding what additive, subtractive, and laser processes can and cannot make, and designing parts to suit the process instead of fighting it — then operating shop equipment safely to produce a working prototype.

Goal: Close the loop from CAD model to physical part — preparing manufacturing-ready files, understanding what each process can and cannot make, and operating shop equipment safely to produce a prototype.
Once a design leaves your screen, the standard is all the maker has. In Unit 1 you saw that there is no single right way to model a part — but there is a shared, standardized way to communicate one. When you hand a file or drawing to a machine shop, a print farm, or a classmate, they cannot ask the model what you meant. They build exactly what the documentation says. Standards exist so that "what the documentation says" and "what you intended" are the same thing.

Why standards carry the design intent

A manufacturer works only from what you send — the geometry, the dimensions, the tolerances, and the notes. Anything you leave to interpretation, they will interpret, and often not the way you hoped. Following accepted standards removes that guesswork.

  • The maker is not the designer. The person cutting the part did not sit in the design reviews. Standard drawing conventions (ASME Y14.5, GD&T, standard thread and surface-finish callouts) are the shared language that lets them read your intent without a phone call.
  • Which dimensions are critical must be stated, not assumed. A tolerance callout tells the shop where to spend effort and where a loose result is fine. Without it they either over-work every feature (cost) or under-work the one that mattered (scrap).
  • Standard file formats preserve meaning across systems. STEP carries clean solid geometry between any CAD and CAM; a well-formed STL is watertight and correctly scaled. A non-standard or sloppy export arrives with gaps, wrong units, or lost features — and the part is made wrong before a tool is even chosen.
  • Ambiguity is expensive and slow. An unclear drawing comes back as an RFI (request for information), a delay, a remake, or a finished part that does not fit its assembly. On a graded prototype, it comes back as a part you cannot use and no time to redo.
  • Standards make a design reviewable and repeatable. A drawing done to convention can be checked by anyone, quoted by multiple shops, and re-run months later with the same result. A drawing full of personal shorthand can only be built by you, standing next to the machine.

The through-line from the first page of this course applies here: capture the design intent, then communicate it in the form everyone downstream already knows how to read.

Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing (GD&T)

Plus/minus tolerances (Unit 2) control how big a feature is, but they say nothing about whether a surface is flat, whether two holes are square to a face, or how a part locates against its neighbor. GD&T is a symbolic language (ASME Y14.5 / ISO GPS) for stating those functional relationships directly on the drawing — it is design intent written in symbols instead of prose. This unit is an introduction: reading the common symbols and understanding what each one asks the manufacturer to control.

Why it beats plain plus/minus: a ± box on four hole dimensions creates a small square tolerance zone and over-constrains the part; a position callout defines a round zone tied to the surfaces that actually matter, allows more good parts through, and tells inspection exactly how to check it.

The building blocks

  • Feature of size: a feature with an axis or centerplane — a hole, a pin, a slot, a width — the things position and orientation controls are applied to
  • Datums: the surfaces or features you designate as the reference for measurement, labeled A, B, C. They are chosen to match how the part actually seats in use — the mounting face, then a locating hole, then a second hole — so the drawing is measured the way the part functions
  • Datum reference frame: primary, secondary, and tertiary datums together lock all six degrees of freedom, giving one repeatable origin everyone measures from
  • Feature control frame: the boxed callout that carries the requirement — read left to right: the geometric characteristic symbol, the tolerance value (with a diameter symbol if the zone is round), then the datums it references
  • Tolerance zone: the geometric space the feature must lie within — a gap between two planes, a cylinder, a band around a profile

The characteristic symbols, by what they control

  • Form (no datum needed): flatness, straightness, circularity, cylindricity — how much a surface may deviate from perfect shape
  • Orientation (needs a datum): perpendicularity, parallelism, angularity — the feature's angle relative to a datum
  • Location (needs datums): position (by far the most common — locates holes and bosses), concentricity, symmetry
  • Profile: profile of a line and profile of a surface — a single control for the form, orientation, and location of curved or irregular shapes
  • Runout (needs a datum axis): circular and total runout — how much a rotating surface wobbles

Material condition modifiers

MMC (maximum material condition, Ⓜ), LMC (Ⓛ), and RFS (the default). When a position tolerance is called at MMC, the part earns extra bonus tolerance as the hole moves away from its worst-case size — which matches how a bolted clearance-hole joint really behaves and lets more usable parts pass.

What to take from this unit: you are not expected to produce a fully GD&T'd drawing yet. You should be able to look at a feature control frame and say what it controls, what it is measured from, and why the designer chose it — and recognize when a functional requirement (parts must mate, a surface must seal, a shaft must run true) calls for a geometric control instead of a plain ± dimension.

Translating parametric models to manufacturing-ready files

Each process consumes a different file. Getting a good part starts with exporting the right one at the right settings.

  • Additive: a watertight mesh (STL or 3MF) exported at a resolution fine enough to hide facets but not so fine the file is unwieldy
  • Laser cutting: a 2D vector profile (DXF) with cut lines and engrave lines on separate layers
  • CNC machining: a solid model (STEP) plus a dimensioned drawing so the machinist knows which dimensions are critical
  • CAM basics: stock setup, toolpath generation, simulation, and the post-processor that turns toolpaths into machine G-code

Additive manufacturing (3D printing)

The course uses material-extrusion printing (FDM / FFF), which builds a part one deposited layer at a time. Understanding how the part is built explains its strengths and its limits.

  • Materials: PLA, PETG, and ABS — printability, strength, and temperature resistance trade-offs
  • Orientation: layers are weakest in the build (Z) direction, so orientation is a strength decision, not just a fit-on-the-bed decision
  • Supports and overhangs: the roughly 45° rule, bridging limits, and designing to avoid support where possible
  • Settings that matter: layer height, wall count, and infill density and pattern
  • Tolerances: holes print undersize, first layers spread — expect to allow for it or drill/ream after

Subtractive manufacturing (CNC)

Subtractive processes start with solid stock and cut material away with a rotating tool. The design constraints come from the tool and how the part is held.

  • Machines: mills, lathes, and routers — what each is shaped to produce
  • Workholding: vises, clamps, and fixtures — the part has to be held rigidly and the tool has to reach the feature
  • Tool access and geometry: internal corners carry the radius of the tool; deep narrow pockets need long thin tools that deflect and chatter
  • Feeds and speeds: the basic idea of matching cutting parameters to the tool and material

Laser cutting

A laser cutter cuts flat sheet by burning or vaporizing a narrow line through it. It's fast and precise in 2D, with its own material and geometry rules.

  • Kerf: the cut removes a finite width of material — it must be accounted for on tight-fitting parts
  • Vector cut vs. raster engrave: line color / layer determines whether the laser cuts through or marks the surface
  • Material limits: wood, acrylic, paper, and cloth cut well; PVC and other chlorinated plastics must never be cut — they release chlorine gas
  • Small-feature limits: minimum hole size and minimum spacing relative to material thickness

DFM design considerations

Design for Manufacturing means shaping the part so the chosen process can make it well, at reasonable cost, without special effort.

  • Match the design to the process: uniform wall thickness for printing and casting; generous internal radii for machining; flat unfolded geometry for laser and sheet work
  • Minimize setups: features on one face are cheaper than features that require re-fixturing the part
  • Tolerance only what matters: tight tolerances drive cost — apply them to mating and functional features, not everything
  • Use standard stock and tooling: standard material sizes, drill sizes, and tool radii cost less and arrive faster
  • Design for the prototype and the product: what's fine for a one-off print may not survive a production process

Machine safety, PPE, and operation

Before running any shop equipment, students are checked out on its safe setup and operation. PPE is required for lab work and is enforced.

  • Baseline: ANSI Z87.1 safety glasses, closed-toe shoes, no loose clothing, jewelry, or long unrestrained hair
  • Rotating machinery: never wear gloves near a spindle, chuck, or bit — a caught glove pulls the hand in
  • Laser cutter: never run unattended, keep a view of the cut, and know where the extraction and fire response are
  • 3D printers: hot nozzle and bed — let them cool, and don't reach into an active machine
  • Before starting: know the location of the e-stop, the fire extinguisher, and the first-aid kit
PPE is not optional. Per the syllabus, a participant without the proper PPE during machine use will be asked to leave the class for that session.
What you'll produce: correct manufacturing-ready files and GD&T documentation for at least one process, and a DFM-reviewed part design ready to hand off to a shop process.
