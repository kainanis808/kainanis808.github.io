Geometry Basics

The shared vocabulary behind every sketch and solid you'll build this semester

CAD software constantly talks to you in the language of geometry — it reports how many faces, edges, and vertices a model has, it asks you to pick an edge to fillet or a face to sketch on, and it measures the area of a profile before it ever becomes a solid. None of that is new math; it's the same handful of ideas from basic geometry, just applied to the parts you design. This page collects that vocabulary in one place — the general shape families you'll sketch and model, the parts a flat (2D) shape is made of, and the parts a solid (3D) shape is made of — so the terms are familiar before Unit 1 builds on them.

Goal: Know the common 2D and 3D shape families by name, and be able to point to a point, edge, vertex, face, and area on an actual shape — the vocabulary CAD software, drawings, and this course all assume you already have.

General shapes: the families you'll sketch and model

Shapes group into families by how they're bounded. A polygon is a flat shape closed entirely by straight edges, named by how many sides it has: a triangle (3), quadrilateral (4 — squares, rectangles, and trapezoids are all quadrilaterals), pentagon (5), hexagon (6), and so on. A polygon is regular when all its sides and angles are equal (a bolt head's hexagon), and irregular otherwise. A circle is bounded by a single curve at a constant distance (the radius) from a center point; an ellipse is its stretched cousin, with two different radii along perpendicular axes.

In 3D, the same idea extends to solids. A polyhedron is a solid bounded entirely by flat polygon faces — a prism (two matching end faces connected by flat sides; a rectangular box is the most common one) or a pyramid (a base that narrows to a single apex point) are the two basic families. A cylinder, cone, and sphere each include at least one curved surface — the solid versions of a circle or ellipse.

Why this matters for CAD: most real parts are combinations of these basic families — a bracket is a prism with holes cut through it; a bolt head is a hexagonal prism; a knob is a cylinder blended into a sphere. Recognizing the families a part is built from is usually the fastest way to decide which sketch and which feature (extrude, revolve, sweep) will create it.

Elements of 2D shapes

Every flat shape, no matter how complex, is made of the same small set of parts. Geometry builds up in dimensions: a point has no size at all (zero-dimensional) — it only marks a location. A line or edge is one-dimensional — it has length, but no width. An area is two-dimensional — it has both length and width, which is why it's measured in squared units (mm², in²).

  • Point: an exact location with no size or shape of its own — just a position
  • Edge (or side): a straight or curved boundary segment connecting two points
  • Vertex: the point where two edges meet — what you'd call a "corner"
  • Area: the amount of surface enclosed by the boundary
  • Perimeter: the total length of the boundary all the way around

Elements of 3D shapes

Add a third dimension and the vocabulary extends by one more level: a flat (or curved) surface is now a face instead of just an outline, and it encloses a volume instead of an area. The point/edge/area relationship from 2D reappears as vertex/edge/face in 3D — same idea, one dimension up.

  • Vertex: a point where edges meet — a corner of the solid
  • Edge: the line segment (straight or curved) where two faces meet
  • Face: a flat or curved surface that bounds the solid
  • Volume: the amount of 3D space enclosed by the faces
  • Surface area: the total area of all the faces added together
A quick check — Euler's formula: for any simple polyhedron (no holes through it), vertices − edges + faces always equals 2 (V − E + F = 2). A cube has 8 vertices, 12 edges, and 6 faces: 8 − 12 + 6 = 2. It's a handy sanity check when you're counting a solid's elements by hand.

2D and 3D vocabulary, side by side

Lining the two sets of terms up side by side makes the pattern obvious — each 3D element is the 2D element with one more dimension added.

2D — flat shapes

  • Point — a location (0D)
  • Edge — a boundary segment (1D)
  • Vertex — where edges meet
  • Area — the enclosed region (2D)
  • Perimeter — total boundary length

3D — solid shapes

  • Vertex — a location (0D)
  • Edge — where two faces meet (1D)
  • Face — a bounding surface (2D)
  • Volume — the enclosed space (3D)
  • Surface area — total area of all faces
The dimensionality ladder: point/vertex (0D) → edge (1D) → area/face (2D) → volume (3D). Each level is built by adding one more direction you can measure in — a point has no size, an edge adds length, a face adds width, and a solid adds depth.
Carry this into Unit 1: a sketch is plane geometry — points, edges, and the area they enclose — and the extrude, revolve, or sweep that turns it into a solid is exactly what adds the third dimension, introducing faces, new edges, and new vertices along the way. When CAD software reports a model's face, edge, and vertex count, or asks you to select an edge to fillet or a face to sketch on, it's using precisely the vocabulary on this page.
