Foundations — Before Unit 1

Design Fundamentals

Defining the problem, design intent, and how a design is organized

Before the sketching, projection, and modeling of Unit 1, it helps to step back and ask what a CAD model is for. A model is not just a picture of a finished shape — it is a record of decisions: what the part has to do, which dimensions matter, and how the pieces of a design depend on one another. This page covers the ideas that run through every later unit: how to define a design problem before solving it, the cyclic design process (why the first version is never the final one), design intent (the reasoning a model should capture), and design organization (top-down vs. bottom-up, and how to structure a design so it stays editable as it grows).

Goal: Understand why models are built the way they are, and why they get rebuilt — so that the sketches, features, and assemblies you create in Units 1–4 are deliberate, robust, and ready to revise, not just visually correct.

The design process is a cycle

Technical design moves from a need to a working prototype through rough stages — but it is not a straight line. You do not think hard enough at the start to get it right and then simply draw it. Every design goes around a loop, and each trip around teaches you something the previous version got wrong.

  • Define: state what the part or product must do, fit, withstand, and cost — the requirements, written before any geometry exists
  • Design: concept options, then the parametric models and drawings that pin down every dimension and tolerance
  • Build: make a prototype — a print, a machined part, a mock-up
  • Test: measure it, fit it to its neighbors, load it, use it the way it will really be used
  • Evaluate: compare what happened against the requirements — what failed, what was close, what a review or a user pointed out
Define→ Design→ Build→ Test→ Evaluate — then back to Design (or Define), with what you learned —

The first prototype is expected to be wrong in ways you could not have predicted from the model alone: a part interferes, a tolerance was too tight, the thing is harder to assemble than it looked on screen, a feature no one needed added cost. That is not failure — that is the process working. The goal of an early iteration is to learn cheaply, so hold designs loosely and expect to revise.

Why this shapes how you model: if you assume the first version is final, you build a model that is painful to change — hard-coded numbers, tangled dependencies, crowded sketches. If you assume you will revise it, you build for iteration: clean sketches, captured design intent, and a readable feature tree. That is what the rest of this page is about.

Define the problem before you design

The first step of the cycle — Define — is the one students skip most often, and it is the one that wastes the most time when skipped. Before any geometry exists you should be able to write down, in a page or less, what the design has to accomplish and how you will know whether it did. A vague goal ("make a good phone stand") cannot be designed toward and cannot be graded; a well-defined one can.

Write goals that are SMART

A useful design goal is SMART: Specific (says exactly what, for whom), Measurable (has a number you can check), Achievable (possible with the time, tools, and skills you have), Relevant (actually serves the need), and Time-bound (due by a defined point). "Hold a phone at a comfortable viewing angle" is a direction; "hold a 600 g phone at 55–70° without tipping, printable on one bed in under 4 hours, done by Week 12" is something you can design to and test against.

Requirements vs. objectives vs. constraints

These three get lumped together as "the spec," but they do different jobs and you handle each differently.

Requirements

What the design must do — pass/fail, non-negotiable functions.

  • "Supports 5 kg without permanent deflection"
  • Either met or not; a design that misses one is not a candidate
  • Verified by test or inspection

Objectives

What you are trying to optimize — directions of "better," used to choose between options that all meet the requirements.

  • "As light as possible," "minimize cost," "fewest parts"
  • Not pass/fail — compared by degree
  • Often trade against each other

Constraints

Fixed limits on the solution space — boundaries you design inside, set by resources or rules.

  • "Fits a 150 mm cube," "$25 material budget," "PLA only," "due Week 12"
  • Not about function — about what is allowed
  • Violating one disqualifies the design
Why the distinction matters: requirements and constraints filter designs to the ones that are acceptable; objectives rank the acceptable ones to find the best. Mix them up and you either reject a good design for missing a "nice to have," or ship one that fails a real must-have.

Decide how you will evaluate success

Define the scorecard before you start designing, so the choice between concepts is made on evidence instead of attachment to whatever you drew first.

  • Turn objectives into criteria: each objective becomes a measurable metric — mass in grams, cost in dollars, part count, assembly time, print time (Unit 1 covers how the model reports mass, volume, and center of gravity directly)
  • Weight the criteria: not every objective matters equally; assign weights that reflect the need
  • Score concepts against the criteria: a decision matrix (or Pugh chart, scoring each option relative to a baseline) makes the comparison explicit and repeatable
  • Check requirements and constraints first: screen those out as pass/fail before scoring — only rank designs that are already acceptable
  • Re-use the scorecard each iteration: the same criteria that pick the concept also tell you, after a prototype, whether the revision actually improved things

Knowing when to stop: the 80/20 rule

The cycle warns that the first design is not good enough. This is the opposite warning: no design is ever perfect, and chasing perfection is its own failure mode. A rough guide is the 80/20 rule — roughly 80% of a design's useful performance comes from the first 20% of the effort, and squeezing out the last stretch of improvement costs far more time than it returns.

  • Move forward once the requirements are met. If the design does what it must do, fits, and is safe, it is ready for the next stage — even if you can see ways it could be nicer
  • The last 20% is optional. Marginal gains in weight, finish, or elegance can be left alone unless a requirement or a customer actually demands them
  • Perpetual designing is a cost. Every extra revision delays the prototype, the test, and everything you would have learned from them — polish on paper is worth less than feedback from a real part
  • "Good enough" is a decision, not a mood. Judge the design against the written requirements, not against an imagined ideal; if it passes, stop and build it
Hold both ideas at once: expect the first version to be wrong and resist the urge to perfect it. The cycle moves forward when a design is good enough to learn from — not when it is flawless, and not before it meets its requirements.

Design intent

Design intent is the reasoning behind a model — what you expect to stay true when a dimension changes. A block with a hole "centered" on its face could be modeled two ways: with the hole dimensioned from one edge, or with the hole tied to the center by a symmetric relation. Both look identical. But widen the block, and only the second one keeps the hole centered. The second model captures the intent; the first only captured a position.

How intent is captured in a parametric model

  • Sketch relations: symmetric, equal, concentric, collinear — these say why geometry is where it is, so it stays right when dimensions change
  • Which dimensions you choose: dimension from the reference that actually matters (a mounting face, a mating edge), not from whatever is convenient
  • Feature order and references: build later features off stable geometry, not off faces that may move or disappear in an edit
  • Equations and linked values: when two dimensions must always match or follow a rule, drive one from the other instead of typing both

Why it matters

  • Editability: a well-built model updates the way you intend; a poorly built one distorts or throws rebuild errors
  • Communication: the next person (or you, months later) can read the feature tree and see the design logic
  • Downstream reuse: assemblies, drawings, and manufacturing files all inherit the model's structure

Organizing a design: top-down vs. bottom-up

When a design has more than one part, you have to decide where the controlling dimensions live. There are two approaches, and most real projects mix them.

Bottom-up

Design each part on its own, fully define it, then bring the finished parts together into an assembly and position them with mates.

  • Parts are independent — editing one does not ripple into others
  • Simple to manage; easy to reuse parts across projects
  • Good for well-understood parts and off-the-shelf or standard hardware
  • Weak when many parts must share dimensions — you end up updating the same number in several files

Top-down

Start from the assembly. Define the shared geometry once — often in a layout sketch or a skeleton part — and derive individual part shapes from those references.

  • Shared dimensions live in one place; a change propagates to every part that references it
  • Keeps mating parts fitting together as the design evolves
  • Good for new designs where the interfaces are still moving
  • More setup, and creates dependencies — an unmanaged web of external references gets fragile

Tools for organizing a top-down design

  • Layout sketch: a single sketch that fixes the key sizes, spacings, and interfaces of the whole assembly before individual parts are modeled
  • Skeleton / master model: a non-manufactured part holding shared reference geometry that other parts point to
  • In-context references: shaping one part directly against another in the assembly — powerful, but track which parts depend on which, and rebuild in the right order
Rule of thumb: use the simplest structure that captures the intent. Reach for top-down only where parts genuinely must share dimensions; keep everything else bottom-up and independent. Every dependency you add is something that can break later.

Practices that keep a design robust

  • Fully define every sketch — an under-defined sketch can drift on an unrelated edit
  • Reference stable geometry — the origin and default planes never move; a filleted edge might vanish
  • Keep sketches simple — one idea per feature; complexity goes into the feature tree, not into one crowded sketch
  • Name and organize features — a tree of "Extrude7, Cut12" tells the next person nothing
  • Minimize dependency chains — the fewer things a feature relies on, the fewer ways it breaks
  • Model the design, not just the shape — if a rule governs the part ("wall is always 2 mm", "these holes match the bolt circle"), build that rule in
Carry this into the units: every portfolio module has stated requirements and constraints — read them as the pass/fail bar, and judge your own work against them the way this page describes; in Unit 1 every sketch you constrain is a design-intent decision; in Unit 3 the choice between building parts first or starting from the assembly is the top-down / bottom-up choice, and the design-review-and-revision step is one turn of the cycle; and in Unit 4 your first prototype will surface something the model missed — a robust feature tree is what lets you go back around the loop without rebuilding from scratch.
