Foundations — Before Unit 1

Design Fundamentals

Design intent, and how a design is organized

Before the sketching, projection, and modeling of Unit 1, it helps to step back and ask what a CAD model is for. A model is not just a picture of a finished shape — it is a record of decisions: what the part has to do, which dimensions matter, and how the pieces of a design depend on one another. This page covers two ideas that run through every later unit: design intent (the reasoning a model should capture) and design organization (top-down vs. bottom-up, and how to structure a design so it stays editable as it grows).

Goal: Understand why models are built the way they are — so that the sketches, features, and assemblies you create in Units 1–4 are deliberate and robust, not just visually correct.

The design process in brief

Technical design moves from a need to a working prototype in rough stages, and it loops back often:

  • Requirements: what the part or product must do, fit, withstand, and cost — stated before any geometry exists
  • Concept: rough options for form and arrangement, chosen against the requirements
  • Detail design: the parametric models and drawings that pin down every dimension and tolerance
  • Prototype and test: build it, measure it, find what the model missed
  • Iterate: revise the model and repeat — parametric CAD exists to make this cheap

CAD sits in the detail-design stage, but the choices you make there decide how painful iteration will be.

Design intent

Design intent is the reasoning behind a model — what you expect to stay true when a dimension changes. A block with a hole "centered" on its face could be modeled two ways: with the hole dimensioned from one edge, or with the hole tied to the center by a symmetric relation. Both look identical. But widen the block, and only the second one keeps the hole centered. The second model captures the intent; the first only captured a position.

How intent is captured in a parametric model

  • Sketch relations: symmetric, equal, concentric, collinear — these say why geometry is where it is, so it stays right when dimensions change
  • Which dimensions you choose: dimension from the reference that actually matters (a mounting face, a mating edge), not from whatever is convenient
  • Feature order and references: build later features off stable geometry, not off faces that may move or disappear in an edit
  • Equations and linked values: when two dimensions must always match or follow a rule, drive one from the other instead of typing both

Why it matters

  • Editability: a well-built model updates the way you intend; a poorly built one distorts or throws rebuild errors
  • Communication: the next person (or you, months later) can read the feature tree and see the design logic
  • Downstream reuse: assemblies, drawings, and manufacturing files all inherit the model's structure

Organizing a design: top-down vs. bottom-up

When a design has more than one part, you have to decide where the controlling dimensions live. There are two approaches, and most real projects mix them.

Bottom-up

Design each part on its own, fully define it, then bring the finished parts together into an assembly and position them with mates.

  • Parts are independent — editing one does not ripple into others
  • Simple to manage; easy to reuse parts across projects
  • Good for well-understood parts and off-the-shelf or standard hardware
  • Weak when many parts must share dimensions — you end up updating the same number in several files

Top-down

Start from the assembly. Define the shared geometry once — often in a layout sketch or a skeleton part — and derive individual part shapes from those references.

  • Shared dimensions live in one place; a change propagates to every part that references it
  • Keeps mating parts fitting together as the design evolves
  • Good for new designs where the interfaces are still moving
  • More setup, and creates dependencies — an unmanaged web of external references gets fragile

Tools for organizing a top-down design

  • Layout sketch: a single sketch that fixes the key sizes, spacings, and interfaces of the whole assembly before individual parts are modeled
  • Skeleton / master model: a non-manufactured part holding shared reference geometry that other parts point to
  • In-context references: shaping one part directly against another in the assembly — powerful, but track which parts depend on which, and rebuild in the right order
Rule of thumb: use the simplest structure that captures the intent. Reach for top-down only where parts genuinely must share dimensions; keep everything else bottom-up and independent. Every dependency you add is something that can break later.

Practices that keep a design robust

  • Fully define every sketch — an under-defined sketch can drift on an unrelated edit
  • Reference stable geometry — the origin and default planes never move; a filleted edge might vanish
  • Keep sketches simple — one idea per feature; complexity goes into the feature tree, not into one crowded sketch
  • Name and organize features — a tree of "Extrude7, Cut12" tells the next person nothing
  • Minimize dependency chains — the fewer things a feature relies on, the fewer ways it breaks
  • Model the design, not just the shape — if a rule governs the part ("wall is always 2 mm", "these holes match the bolt circle"), build that rule in
Carry this into the units: in Unit 1 every sketch you constrain is a design-intent decision; in Unit 3 the choice between building parts first or starting from the assembly is the top-down / bottom-up choice; and in Units 3–4 a robust feature tree is what lets you revise a design after a review or a failed prototype without rebuilding it from scratch.
