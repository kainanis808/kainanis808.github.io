Unit 3 — Weeks 11–13

Design for Assembly (DFA)

Building and documenting assemblies

Most products are more than one part. This unit is about putting part models together into a working assembly, documenting that assembly the way a manufacturer expects, and — just as important — applying the design principles that decide whether the finished product is quick or painful to put together. A design that models fine can still be a bad design if it takes twice as long to assemble as it needs to.

Goal: Combine part models into working assemblies, document them the way a manufacturer expects, and apply the design principles that make an assembly easier and cheaper to put together.

Assembly modeling with mates and constraints

An assembly positions components relative to one another using mates (also called constraints). Each mate removes degrees of freedom until a part is either fully located or deliberately left free to move.

  • Common mates: coincident, concentric, parallel, perpendicular, distance, angle, and tangent
  • First component: the first part placed is fixed and becomes the ground the rest is built against
  • Degrees of freedom: a free rigid body has six; mates remove them one axis at a time
  • Motion: leaving rotational or linear freedom on purpose so a mechanism can be moved and checked

Building assemblies from part models

The standard workflow is bottom-up: model each part on its own, then bring the finished parts into an assembly and mate them. Top-down techniques — driving part geometry from the assembly — are introduced as well.

  • Sub-assemblies: grouping components that ship or install as a unit, keeping the top-level tree manageable
  • Component patterns and mirrors: reusing repeated parts instead of placing each one by hand
  • Managing references: keeping file links intact so the assembly updates when a part changes

Applying fasteners and threads within assemblies

Hardware is added from a standard library rather than modeled from scratch. This keeps every bolt, nut, and washer consistent and correctly sized.

  • Library hardware: pulling standard fasteners from a parts library (Toolbox or equivalent)
  • Hole alignment: mating fasteners to holes and using hole series / hole wizard features so clearance, tap, and counterbore holes propagate through the stack
  • Reusable components: configuring one fastener for multiple lengths and sizes

Methods of joining

How two parts are held together is a design decision with real consequences for cost, strength, serviceability, and how the product is assembled. Threaded fasteners are only one option, and often not the best one. Choose the joining method deliberately, then design both parts to suit it.

  • Threaded fasteners into tapped holes: the default for metal-to-metal — strong, standard, and removable. Needs enough material for thread engagement (roughly 1.5–2× the screw diameter) and access for a tool.
  • Heat-set threaded inserts: the standard way to put a durable machine thread into a plastic or 3D-printed part. A brass insert is pushed into a molded or printed boss with a soldering iron; the melted plastic flows into its knurls and locks it. Design the boss to the insert manufacturer's hole diameter and depth, add a lead-in chamfer, and keep enough wall around it. Far more reusable than screwing directly into plastic or using self-tapping screws.
  • Snap-fit joints: a molded or printed feature that deflects during assembly and springs back to hold — cantilever hooks, annular (ring) snaps, and torsion snaps. No hardware, no tools, fast assembly. Design for the material's allowable strain, add lead-in angles, and decide whether it should be releasable (serviceable) or permanent (higher retention angle).
  • Press / interference fits: holding by friction alone from a slight size mismatch — dowel pins, bushings, bearings. Cheap and precise, but hard to take apart and sensitive to tolerance.
  • Compliant mechanisms: parts that get their motion or retention from the flexibility of the material itself rather than from separate hinges or springs — living hinges, flexure pivots, and one-piece clips. They reduce part count to the extreme (often to one), remove assembly entirely, and have no pin joints to wear — at the cost of limited travel and fatigue life, and needing a material that tolerates repeated flexing (PP and PE for living hinges).
  • Permanent joints: adhesives, solvent welding, ultrasonic welding, riveting — used where the joint never needs to come apart. Commit to these only when serviceability truly is not required.

The DFA trade-off: hardware is universal and serviceable but adds parts, tools, and assembly time; snap-fits and compliant features add almost nothing to the parts list but must be designed carefully and are harder to revise once the tooling or print is dialed in.

Using online component repositories

You should not model a part you can download. Standard hardware, bearings, motors, extrusion, pneumatics, connectors, and countless other catalog items are available as ready-made CAD models from online repositories — and using them is normal professional practice, not a shortcut.

  • Where the models come from: McMaster-Carr (download a STEP or native model straight from the product page of almost any item it sells), manufacturer sites, and aggregators such as TraceParts, GrabCAD, and 3D ContentCentral
  • Why it matters for DFA: a downloaded model is the real catalog part — correct dimensions, correct thread, a real part number you can put on the BOM and actually order. Your assembly is then built around hardware that exists, at the price and lead time listed.
  • It reinforces "design with standard parts": if the exact fastener, bracket, or bearing is one click away, there is little reason to invent a custom one — which is the DFA principle of using standard, off-the-shelf components
  • Bring them in cleanly: downloaded models arrive as imported (dumb) solids with no feature history — keep them in a dedicated library folder, do not edit their geometry, and mate to them like any other component
  • Check before you trust: confirm units and scale on import, simplify or use a "simplified" configuration if the model is too heavy, and verify the part number matches what you intend to buy

Assembly drawings, exploded views, and BOMs

An assembly drawing shows how the parts go together, not how to make each one. Its job is to communicate sequence, orientation, and what hardware goes where.

  • Exploded view: components pulled apart along assembly paths, with explode lines showing where each goes
  • Balloons: numbered callouts keyed to the parts list
  • Bill of materials (BOM): item number, part number, description, and quantity — generated from and linked to the assembly
  • Assembly vs. installation drawings: what belongs on each and the level of detail expected

DFA design principles

Design for Assembly is a set of rules for reducing the time, cost, and error rate of putting a product together. The biggest lever is part count.

  • Minimize part count: a part is a candidate for elimination unless it moves relative to its neighbors, must be a different material, or must be removable for service
  • Prefer off-the-shelf parts: a cataloged component — with a downloadable model and a real part number — is almost always cheaper, faster, and more reliable than a custom part you design and fabricate
  • Design for easy insertion: chamfers and lead-ins, self-locating features, and parts that can't be installed backwards
  • Avoid reorientation: assemble from one direction, ideally top-down, so the workpiece isn't flipped
  • Standardize fasteners: fewer unique sizes means fewer tools and fewer mistakes
  • Symmetry: make parts either fully symmetric so orientation doesn't matter, or clearly asymmetric so the correct orientation is obvious
  • Tolerance stack-up: checking that the accumulated tolerances of mating parts still allow assembly and function — worked through in the example below

Worked example: a tolerance stack-up

Three spacer blocks — A, B, and C — drop into a milled pocket in a housing. They need a small end gap so the cover can close. The gap must never be negative (the stack would jam and the cover would not seat) and should stay under about 1.2 mm (any looser and the stack rattles).

Dimensions from the part drawings. The pocket opens the gap (+); each block closes it (−).

Pocket depth+60.00±0.2059.8060.20

Block C−24.50±0.1524.3524.65
Total tolerance (sum of the ± values)±0.55

Step 1 — nominal gap

gapnom = 60.00 − (20.00 + 15.00 + 24.50) = 0.50 mm. Looks fine on its own.

Step 2 — worst-case (arithmetic) stack

The gap is largest when the pocket is at its maximum and every block is at its minimum; smallest when the pocket is at its minimum and every block is at its maximum.

  • Largest gap = 60.20 − (19.90 + 14.90 + 24.35) = 60.20 − 59.15 = +1.05 mm
  • Smallest gap = 59.80 − (20.10 + 15.10 + 24.65) = 59.80 − 59.85 = −0.05 mm
  • Shortcut: gap = nominal ± total tolerance = 0.50 ± 0.55 → the same −0.05 … +1.05 mm
Verdict: the nominal gap is fine, but the worst-case minimum is −0.05 mm — a possible interference. A fraction of assemblies built from in-tolerance parts would not go together. The stack fails the check.

Step 3 — fix it (choose one)

  • Shift a nominal: make Block C 24.30 → gap = 0.70 ± 0.55 = 0.15 … 1.25 mm. Minimum now positive.
  • Tighten a tolerance: hold the pocket to ±0.10 → total ±0.45, gap = 0.50 ± 0.45 = 0.05 … 0.95 mm.
  • Absorb the variation: add a shim or a wave washer so the stack does not depend on the tolerances lining up.

Step 4 — statistical (RSS) check

For a long chain, worst-case is often too conservative — every part sitting at its extreme in the same direction is unlikely. Root-sum-square (RSS) combines the tolerances as independent random variables:

TRSS = √(0.20² + 0.10² + 0.10² + 0.15²) = √0.0825 ≈ 0.29 mm
gap ≈ 0.50 ± 0.29 → 0.21 … 0.79 mm (treating each ± value as about 3σ)

So statistically an interference is very unlikely — but RSS is a probability, not a guarantee. Use worst-case when a single jammed assembly is unacceptable (small production runs, safety-critical fits); use RSS for long stacks in volume production where a small, known reject rate is acceptable.

Two rules for building the chain: only dimensions along the same line add to a stack, and you must track direction — a feature that opens the gap counts +, one that closes it counts −. Baseline dimensioning (measuring every feature from one datum instead of chaining feature-to-feature) keeps the chain short and the stack small.

Design review and iteration

The unit ends with a workshop: present an assembly, take feedback against the DFA principles above, and produce a revised version. The deliverable is not just the new model but a clear record of what changed and why.

What you'll produce: a multi-part assembly model with correct mates, an exploded assembly drawing with balloons and a linked BOM, and a documented design revision showing a measurable DFA improvement.
