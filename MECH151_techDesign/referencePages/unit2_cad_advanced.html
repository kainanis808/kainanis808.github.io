Unit 2 — Weeks 7–10

CAD Advanced

Detailed views, fasteners, and production drawings

A basic model shows the shape of a part. A production drawing tells a shop exactly how to make it — including the internal features a plain outside view can't show, the threads and hardware it uses, and how much each dimension is allowed to vary. Unit 2 adds the specialized views, annotation practices, and file settings that turn a model into a document someone else can build from.

Goal: Move from a basic model to a documentation-ready one — the specialized views, thread and fastener callouts, tolerancing, and title-block practices that make a drawing something a shop can build from.
The model has infinite valid approaches; the drawing has shared conventions. How you build the underlying part is still open — there is no single correct feature tree. But a production drawing is read by people who did not make it, so the annotation side follows accepted standards (ASME Y14.5, ISO): which views to show, how to place dimensions, how to call out threads and tolerances. Think of it as freedom in how you model, discipline in how you document — and even then, two correct drawings of the same part can still differ in view choice and layout.

Section views

A section view slices the part with an imaginary cutting plane and shows what's inside, replacing a tangle of hidden lines with a clear cut face. The cut surface is marked with section lining (hatching).

  • Full section: the cutting plane passes entirely through the part
  • Half section: one quarter removed — shows inside and outside on one view, good for symmetric parts
  • Offset section: the cutting plane is stepped to pass through features that don't line up
  • Broken-out, revolved, and removed sections: local sections used where a full cut isn't needed
  • Conventions: ribs, webs, fasteners, shafts, and pins are not section-lined even when the plane passes through them

Auxiliary views

When a face is inclined, it appears foreshortened in every principal view — you can't measure it or see its true shape. An auxiliary view is projected perpendicular to that face so it appears true-size. This is the descriptive-geometry theory from Unit 1 put to use.

  • Primary auxiliary: projected from one principal view to show one inclined surface true-shape
  • Partial auxiliary: only the inclined feature is drawn — the rest is omitted to avoid distortion and clutter
  • Reference-plane method: measurements are transferred from a principal view along projection lines

Threads and fastener representation

Threads are almost never drawn in full detail — a standardized representation plus a note carries all the information a shop needs.

  • Thread terms:
    • Major / minor diameter — the largest diameter of the thread, measured across the crests, and the smallest, measured across the roots
    • Pitch — the distance from one thread crest to the next
    • Lead — the axial distance the thread advances in one full turn; equal to the pitch on a standard single-start thread
    • External vs. internal — a thread on a shaft (a bolt or screw) versus a thread inside a hole (a nut or tapped hole)
    • Right- vs. left-hand — the direction you turn it to tighten; right-hand is the default
    • Class of fit — a numbered grade for how tightly the internal and external threads mate (1A/2A/3A for inch, 6g/6H for metric); a higher number is a tighter fit
  • Representation: detailed, schematic, and simplified — simplified is most common on production drawings
  • Thread notes: Unified inch (e.g. 1/4-20 UNC-2A) and metric (e.g. M6×1), including class and length of engagement
  • Fasteners: bolts, screws, nuts, washers, and inserts — how they're called out and how clearance and tap-drill holes are sized

Production and detail drawings

A detail drawing is a controlled document. Its format carries information that isn't in the geometry: who made it, at what revision, to what standard, and with what default tolerances.

  • Title block: part name and number, material, scale, units, drafter, date, and sheet size
  • Revision block: a dated history of every change to the drawing
  • Tolerance block: default tolerances applied to any dimension without its own
  • General notes and BOM: finish, deburring, and heat-treat notes; a parts list for multi-part drawings
  • Sheet formatting: standard sheet sizes (A–E / A4–A0), scale callouts, and multi-sheet drawings

Introduction to tolerancing

No part is made to an exact dimension. Tolerancing states how much variation is acceptable so that parts still fit and function.

  • Methods: limit dimensions and plus/minus tolerances — bilateral or unilateral
  • Fits: clearance (always a gap), interference (always an overlap — a press fit), and transition (could be either) fits between mating parts. The allowance is that tightest intended condition
  • Standard fit designations: ISO hole-basis and shaft-basis callouts such as H7/g6 (hole H7, shaft g6 — a close clearance fit)
  • Tolerance accumulation: how chained dimensions stack up, and why baseline dimensioning limits it

CAD software configuration

Consistent output comes from configured software, not from fixing every file by hand. This topic covers setting up the environment once so every part, assembly, and drawing starts correct.

  • Templates: part, assembly, and drawing templates with the right units, drafting standard, and title block
  • Document properties: units, dimensioning standard (ASME / ISO), grid, and layer settings
  • Custom properties: file metadata that auto-populates the title block and BOM
  • File management: naming, folder structure, and keeping references between parts, assemblies, and drawings intact

CAD/CAM data translation

Moving a model between systems — CAD to CAM, or one CAD package to another — usually means exporting to a neutral format. Each format keeps some information and loses the rest.

  • Solid exchange formats: STEP (AP203 / AP214 / AP242) and IGES — geometry is preserved, but the parametric feature history is not
  • Kernel formats: Parasolid and ACIS — used between packages that share a modeling kernel
  • Mesh formats: STL and 3MF — the model becomes a faceted surface with no true curves; resolution is set at export
  • Translation issues: lost or corrupted faces, gaps requiring geometry healing, and unit-scale errors
What you'll produce: a fully detailed production drawing that includes section and auxiliary views, thread and fastener callouts, a complete title block, and dimensional tolerances — plus correctly configured templates and a clean model export.
