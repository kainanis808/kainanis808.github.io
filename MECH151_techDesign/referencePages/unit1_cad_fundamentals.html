Unit 1 — Weeks 4–7

CAD Fundamentals (Basic)

Sketching, views, and the first parametric model

Everything you build later in this course — detailed drawings, assemblies, manufacturing files — rests on the skills in this unit. A 3D model is only as good as the sketch it grows from, and a drawing is only useful if it follows the projection and dimensioning conventions everyone else reads it by. Unit 1 covers how to control a sketch, how to project a part into standard views, and how to turn a sketch into a parametric solid that updates when you change it.

Goal: Build the drawing and modeling foundation every later unit depends on — laying out a controlled sketch, projecting a part into standard views, and creating a parametric part model.
There is no single "right" way to model a part. The same shape can be built from a different base feature, a different set of sketches, a different feature order — and there are effectively infinite valid combinations. What separates a good model from a poor one is not matching some canonical answer; it is whether the model captures the design intent, rebuilds without errors, and stays easy for the next person to edit. Two classmates can hand in completely different feature trees and both be correct. Focus on making deliberate choices you can explain, not on guessing the "expected" one.

Sketching fundamentals

A sketch is a 2D profile drawn on a plane or flat face — it becomes the input to a 3D feature such as an extrude or a revolve. Good sketches are simple: draw only the geometry the feature needs, keep profiles closed, and put complexity into separate features rather than one crowded sketch.

  • Sketch entities: lines, arcs, circles, rectangles, slots, splines, and points
  • Reference geometry: the origin, the three default planes (front, top, right), and construction lines that guide geometry without becoming part of the profile
  • Closed vs. open profiles: a closed loop can be extruded into a solid; an open profile is used for thin features, surfaces, or paths

Geometry of shapes: 2D construction

Before a shape is a sketch, it is a piece of plane geometry — a set of points, lines, and curves in defined relationships. Knowing those relationships lets you build a shape from its geometric conditions instead of nudging entities until they look right.

  • Basic elements: point, line (and line segment), circle, circular arc, ellipse, and free-form curve (spline)
  • Circle and arc geometry: defined by center and radius/diameter; an arc adds a start and end angle. A chord connects two points on a circle; a central angle subtends an arc
  • Polygons: regular polygons are defined by a center and either an inscribed circle (flat-to-flat, "across flats") or a circumscribed circle (point-to-point, "across corners") — which one matters for hex features and bolt heads
  • Tangency: a line or arc is tangent to a circle when it touches at exactly one point and is perpendicular to the radius there. Tangent arc-to-arc and line-to-arc transitions are how rounded shapes stay smooth
  • Fillets and chamfers in a sketch: a fillet is a tangent arc that replaces a sharp corner; a chamfer is a straight cut across it
  • Constructions: midpoint, perpendicular bisector, angle bisector, and offset (a parallel copy at a fixed distance) — the classic geometric constructions, now done by relations rather than compass and straightedge
  • Symmetry and centerlines: building half a shape about a construction line and mirroring it guarantees the two halves stay identical

The relations listed in the next section (tangent, concentric, equal, symmetric…) are the vocabulary for stating these geometric conditions so the CAD system holds them for you.

Sketch constraints and relations

Constraints lock down where sketch geometry can move. There are two kinds: geometric relations (coincident, collinear, parallel, perpendicular, tangent, concentric, equal, horizontal, vertical, symmetric) and dimensions (linear, radial, diameter, angular). Together they remove degrees of freedom until the sketch is fully defined.

  • Under-defined: geometry can still be dragged — the sketch is not fully controlled
  • Fully defined: every entity is fixed by relations and dimensions; this is the target state before finishing a sketch
  • Over-defined: conflicting or redundant constraints — must be resolved, not ignored

Adding relations before dimensions keeps a sketch stable: relations capture the design intent (this hole stays centered, these edges stay equal) so that changing one dimension updates the model predictably instead of distorting it.

Orthographic projection and multiview theory

Orthographic projection flattens a 3D object into 2D views by projecting each face straight onto a viewing plane. Imagine the part inside a glass box; each face of the box catches one view. Unfolding the box gives the standard arrangement of up to six principal views, though most parts need only three: front, top, and right side.

  • Third-angle projection: the U.S. standard — the top view sits above the front view, the right-side view sits to its right
  • Line conventions: visible edges as solid lines, hidden edges as dashed lines, and centerlines for holes and axes of symmetry
  • View selection: choose the front view that shows the most shape and the fewest hidden lines, then add only the views needed to describe the part fully

Descriptive geometry basics

Descriptive geometry is the theory behind projecting views: how points, lines, and planes appear from different directions, and how to construct a view that shows a feature's true size and shape. It is the groundwork for the auxiliary views covered in Unit 2.

  • True length of a line: seen only when the line is parallel to the viewing plane
  • Point view of a line / edge view of a plane: seen when looking along the line or plane
  • True shape of a plane: seen only in a view projected perpendicular to that plane

Primary and secondary views

Views are projected in sequence. A primary view is projected directly from a principal view (front, top, or side). A secondary view is projected from a primary view to reveal a feature that is still not shown true-size — for example, an inclined face that is skewed in every principal view. Each step rotates the line of sight until the feature of interest is seen straight on.

Basic dimensioning standards

Dimensions tell the reader how big the part is and how precisely each feature must be made. They follow accepted conventions (ASME Y14.5 in the U.S.) so that any shop reads them the same way.

  • Anatomy: extension lines, dimension lines, arrowheads, and the dimension value — kept off the part outline and out of the view
  • Placement: dimension to visible outlines, not hidden lines; group related dimensions; keep the smallest dimensions closest to the view
  • Do not over-dimension: each feature is located once — a duplicate or redundant dimension creates conflict on the shop floor
  • Feature callouts: diameter, radius, counterbore, countersink, and depth symbols for holes

Geometry of shapes: building 3D solids

A 3D shape is either a primitive volume or a combination of them. Every solid model is really a running total: start with a volume, add more, and cut some away.

  • Primitives: the block (rectangular prism), cylinder, sphere, cone, and torus — the simple volumes more complex parts are reducible to
  • Profile-based features turn a 2D sketch into a volume by a rule of motion:
    • Extrude — push a profile straight along an axis (a prism)
    • Revolve — spin a profile around an axis (anything round: shafts, bottles, wheels)
    • Sweep — run a profile along a path (tubing, handles, trim)
    • Loft — blend between two or more profiles (transitions, blended shapes)
  • Boolean operations combine volumes: union / add (boss), subtract / cut (pocket, hole), and intersect (keep only the shared volume)
  • Applied (dress-up) features: fillet, chamfer, shell, draft, and pattern — operations on an existing solid rather than a new sketched volume

Introduction to parametric part modeling

Parametric modeling builds a solid as a history of features, each driven by dimensions and relations. Change a dimension and the whole model rebuilds to match. This is what makes CAD editable rather than something you redraw from scratch.

  • Base feature: the first solid, usually an extrude or revolve of a fully defined sketch
  • Added features: extrude, cut, revolve, fillet, chamfer, hole, shell, pattern — each added in order on the feature tree
  • Design intent: building features and relations so the model updates the way you intend when a dimension changes
  • Sketch planes and references: choosing where each feature's sketch lives so edits stay stable

Parametric modeling practice

Practice focuses on editing an existing model without breaking it: changing driving dimensions, reordering or suppressing features, fixing rebuild errors, and recognizing when a model's design intent was set up poorly. The skill being built is reading a feature tree and predicting what an edit will do.

Geometry of shapes: measurable properties

Every shape carries numbers that follow from its geometry, and CAD computes them from the model directly. These are the values you check a design against — the measurable criteria from the Design Fundamentals page.

  • Of a 2D profile: area, perimeter, centroid (the geometric center), second moment of area (area moment of inertia, I — resistance to bending), and section modulus (I divided by distance to the extreme fiber — used directly in bending-stress checks)
  • Of a 3D solid: volume, surface area, and — once a material is assigned — mass (volume × density), center of mass / center of gravity, and the mass moment of inertia (resistance to angular acceleration)
  • Why they matter: mass drives cost and shipping and is often a hard requirement; the center of gravity decides whether a part balances, tips, or sits where you need it; area and mass moments of inertia decide how stiff a section is and how it responds to load and vibration
  • Garbage in, garbage out: the numbers are only right if the material density is set correctly and the model is a single watertight solid — an open or multi-body model gives a meaningless mass
What you'll produce: a set of fully defined sketches, a multiview orthographic drawing with correct line types and dimensions, and your first fully parametric part model with a clean, editable feature tree.
