What Metallurgy Is & Why We Use Alloys Process

What it is

Metallurgy is the science of metals — their structure, properties, and behavior — so they can be chosen, tested, treated, and worked effectively. An alloy is an intentional mixture of two or more elements engineered for desired properties; nearly every engineering metal is an alloy.

How it works

Adding a second element disrupts the host metal's crystal lattice, which blocks deformation and changes properties. The first alloy, bronze, adds ~10–20% tin to copper, making it much harder and stronger than either metal alone while keeping copper's workability and corrosion resistance. The same idea carries through the whole course: carbon in iron makes steel, chromium in steel makes stainless.

When you'll apply it

Any time you read a material callout, you're reading an alloy designation. Knowing that properties come from deliberate composition lets you understand why a substitute material that "looks the same" may not perform the same.

The Atom & Valence Electrons Concept

What it is

Atoms are made of protons (positive, in the nucleus — they define the element), neutrons (no charge, in the nucleus — they set the mass; atoms with different neutron counts are isotopes), and electrons (negative, in shells around the nucleus). Valence electrons are the outermost electrons and the only ones involved in bonding.

When you'll apply it

Valence electrons explain how metals bond and conduct, and why small additions matter — carbon's four valence electrons let it bond in four directions and sit between iron atoms, blocking deformation. This is the root of steel's strength and comes back in corrosion (Week 16), where metals give up electrons.

The Periodic Table — Groups, Periods & Classification Concept

What it is

Periods (rows) add an electron shell each time you move down; groups (columns) share the same number of valence electrons and therefore similar bonding behavior. The staircase line separates metals (left — conductive, malleable, ductile, lustrous), metalloids (on the line — semiconducting), and non-metals (right — poor conductors, brittle or gaseous).

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use it to locate the engineering metals (Fe, Cu, Ni, Cr, Ti, Mo — the transition metals in Groups 3–12) and to predict general behavior of an unfamiliar element listed on a material certificate.

Atomic Bonding — Metallic, Ionic & Covalent Process

What it is

The octet rule says atoms bond to reach a stable 8 valence electrons. Metallic bonds (metal + metal) share electrons in a free-flowing "electron sea"; ionic bonds (metal + non-metal) transfer electrons, forming strongly attracting ions; covalent bonds (non-metal + non-metal) share electrons in strong, directional bonds.

How it works

In a metallic bond, the electron sea lets atoms slide past one another without breaking the bond — which is why metals conduct heat and electricity and bend rather than shatter. Ionic and covalent bonds lock electrons in place, giving high melting points and hardness but brittleness.

When you'll apply it

Explains why metals can be formed and welded, why ceramics (oxides, carbides) on a metal surface or as inclusions are hard and brittle, and why conductivity is used in eddy current testing and corrosion.

Alloys & Solid Solutions Process

What it is

A solid solution is an alloy in which the added element dissolves into the host's crystal lattice. In a substitutional solid solution, alloying atoms of similar size replace host atoms (Cu-Ni, brass, Cr and Ni in stainless). In an interstitial solid solution, much smaller atoms fit into the gaps between host atoms (carbon in iron).

How it works

Substitution works when atoms are within about 15% of each other in size; the lattice keeps its structure but is strained. Interstitial atoms squeeze into gaps without displacing host atoms — even tiny amounts stiffen the lattice and block slip, which is why a fraction of a percent of carbon changes iron so dramatically.

When you'll apply it

Used whenever you interpret composition on a data sheet or MTR — it tells you which elements are strengthening the metal and why small chemistry deviations can push a part out of spec.

Crystal Structures — Unit Cells (SC, BCC, FCC, HCP) Concept

What it is

When metals solidify, atoms arrange into a repeating 3D crystal lattice. The unit cell is its smallest repeating building block. The four common structures are simple cubic (~52% packed, teaching example only), BCC (~68%; ferritic iron, Cr, W, Mo), FCC (~74%; Al, Cu, Ni, austenite — most ductile), and HCP (~74%; Ti, Mg, Zn — fewer slip planes, less ductile). The atomic packing factor is the fraction of cell volume occupied by atoms.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Crystal structure predicts behavior on the job: FCC metals stay tough at low temperature, BCC steels can turn brittle in the cold (Week 7, DBTT), and the BCC↔FCC change in iron is what makes heat treating steel possible (Week 4).

Grains & Grain Boundaries Process

What it is

A grain is a single crystal domain — millions of unit cells with the same orientation. A grain boundary is where two differently oriented grains meet. Real metals are polycrystalline: countless grains packed together, each pointing a different direction.

How it works

Grain boundaries block dislocation movement (the way deformation spreads through metal). More boundaries means more barriers, so smaller grains produce a stronger, tougher material.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Grain size shows up under the microscope in metallography, governs strength (Hall-Petch, Week 2), and grain boundaries are where sensitization, intergranular corrosion, and hydrogen trapping happen later in the course.

Stress & Strain Process

What it is

Stress (σ = F/A) is internal force per unit area, reported in Pa, MPa, or ksi. Strain (ε = ΔL/L₀) is deformation relative to original size and is dimensionless (or %). Stress can be tensile (pulling apart), compressive (pushing together), or shear (layers sliding parallel to a surface).

How it works

Divide the applied load by the cross-sectional area to get stress; divide the change in length by the original length to get strain. Hooke's Law (σ = E·ε) links them in the elastic range, where E is the elastic (Young's) modulus.

When you'll apply it

Every strength value on a data sheet, every tensile test, and every design load is expressed in stress. You'll calculate it whenever you check whether a part, fastener, or weld is loaded below its allowable value.

Elastic vs. Plastic Deformation (Yield, UTS & Necking) Process

What it is

Elastic deformation is recoverable — bonds stretch and spring back. Plastic deformation is permanent. The yield strength is the stress where permanent deformation begins; the proportional limit is the last point where stress and strain are linear; the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) is the peak of the stress-strain curve, where necking (local reduction in cross-section) begins. The elastic modulus is the slope of the elastic region (stiffness).

How it works

Below yield, atoms are displaced but return. Above yield, dislocations move through the crystal so the metal changes shape without fracturing. After UTS, deformation concentrates in the neck until fracture.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Yield strength is the most important value for structural design — an over-torqued bolt that "clicks" has yielded and won't hold preload. Use these points to read stress-strain curves, data sheets, and test reports.

Key Mechanical Properties — Strength, Hardness, Ductility, Toughness, Brittleness Concept

What it is

Strength: resistance to yielding or fracture. Hardness: resistance to localized surface indentation. Ductility: ability to deform plastically before fracture (% elongation, % reduction in area). Toughness: energy absorbed before fracture (area under the stress-strain curve; requires strength and ductility). Brittleness: fracture with little or no plastic deformation — the opposite of toughness.

When you'll apply it

These five words are how materials are specified and compared. Use them to choose a material for a job (formable sheet needs ductility; an impact-loaded part needs toughness; a wear surface needs hardness) and to know which test verifies each (tensile, hardness, Charpy/Izod).

Point Defects — Vacancies, Interstitials & Substitutionals Process

What it is

A point defect is a disruption at a single lattice site: a vacancy (missing atom), an interstitial (extra atom squeezed into a gap), or a substitutional (a different atom occupying a lattice site).

How it works

Vacancies increase exponentially with temperature and give atoms room to move (diffusion). Interstitial carbon and substitutional Cr, Ni, and Mo distort the lattice and block dislocations — the mechanism behind carbon and alloy steels.

When you'll apply it

Point defects explain why heat treatment and annealing work at all, and why composition changes properties.

Line Defects — Dislocations (Edge, Screw, Mixed) Process

What it is

A dislocation is a line defect that lets metal deform plastically by shifting atoms into new bonds a row at a time instead of breaking every bond at once. An edge dislocation is an extra half-plane of atoms; a screw dislocation spirals around the dislocation line; most real dislocations are mixed. The Burgers vector describes the size and direction of the lattice distortion.

How it works

Like moving a wrinkle across a rug instead of dragging the whole rug, a dislocation glides along a slip plane under stress. Edge dislocations move in the direction of applied stress; screw dislocations move by shearing and can cross-slip between planes. Anything that blocks dislocation motion — grain boundaries, alloy atoms, other dislocations — makes the metal stronger.

When you'll apply it

Dislocations are the "why" behind ductility, work hardening (Week 11), and nearly every strengthening method. A metal with zero dislocations would be glass-like and fail without warning.

Planar Defects — Grain Boundaries, Twins & Stacking Faults Process

What it is

Planar defects are 2D surfaces where crystal orientation or stacking changes. Grain boundaries can be high-angle (>15°, high energy, more reactive) or low-angle. Twin boundaries are mirror-image boundaries (annealing twins in FCC metals; deformation twins in HCP/BCC under shock or cold). A stacking fault breaks the normal ABCABC (FCC) or ABAB (HCP) stacking sequence; its stacking fault energy (SFE) controls cold-working behavior.

How it works

Low SFE gives wide faults that stop cross-slip, so the metal work-hardens quickly; high SFE allows easy cross-slip and softer cold-working behavior.

When you'll apply it

Helps you interpret microstructures (twins are a recognizable feature in annealed copper and austenitic stainless) and anticipate how fast a metal will harden during forming.

Volume Defects — Voids, Porosity, Inclusions, Cracks, Delamination & Segregation Concept

What it is

3D imperfections large enough to find with inspection equipment. Voids/porosity: empty cavities from gas or shrinkage. Inclusions: trapped non-metallic particles (oxides, sulfides, slag). Cracks: planar separations — the most dangerous defect class. Delamination: separation between rolled/forged layers. Segregation: uneven distribution of alloying elements from solidification.

When you'll apply it

These are the defects that cause service failures. Each one points to an NDT method: RT/CT for porosity, PT/MT/UT/RT for cracks, UT for delaminations. You'll meet them again as casting, forming, weld, and PM defects in Units 3–5.

Grain Boundary Strengthening & Annealing (Hall-Petch; Recovery, Recrystallization, Grain Growth) Process

What it is

The Hall-Petch relationship, σᵧ = σ₀ + k/√d, states that yield strength rises as grain size (d) shrinks. Annealing after cold work proceeds in three stages: recovery, recrystallization, and grain growth.

How it works

Dislocations can't cross a grain boundary, so they pile up and create back-stress; smaller grains mean more pile-ups and higher strength. During annealing: (1) recovery relieves internal stress with little change in grain shape; (2) recrystallization nucleates new strain-free grains, dropping strength and restoring ductility; (3) grain growth coarsens the new grains if held too long — so time and temperature are controlled to hit a target grain size.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Use it when a part has been cold-worked and needs its ductility restored, when reading an anneal specification, or when explaining why a weld HAZ or over-annealed part lost strength from grain coarsening.

Diffusion — Atoms in Motion Process

What it is

Diffusion is the movement of atoms through a solid. Substitutional diffusion (large atoms like Cr, Ni, Mo) requires a neighboring vacancy and is slow; interstitial diffusion (small atoms like C, N, H) moves through the gaps between atoms and is fast.

How it works

Atoms vibrate constantly; with enough thermal energy they jump to an adjacent vacancy or interstitial gap. The rate increases exponentially with temperature, so a small temperature change produces a large change in diffusion.

When you'll apply it

Diffusion drives heat treatment, carburizing and nitriding (Week 4), sintering (Week 13), sensitization (Week 5), and hydrogen cracking (Week 15). It's why heat-treat temperatures and hold times must be controlled precisely.

Work Hardening, Flow Stress & Fracture Toughness (Introduction) Concept

What it is

Work hardening is strengthening from dislocations accumulating during cold work. Flow stress is the true stress needed to keep a metal deforming, described by the Hollomon equation σ = K·εⁿ. Fatigue is failure below yield strength from repeated cyclic loading; the endurance limit is the stress below which steel can survive indefinitely. Fracture toughness (KIC) measures resistance to crack growth from an existing flaw.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

These ideas preview Units 3–5: fatigue testing (Week 7), forming and %CW (Week 11), and fatigue fracture analysis (Week 15). Fracture toughness tells you how large a crack a part can tolerate before sudden failure.

Unit 1 wrap-up — revisiting the driving question

What is happening inside a metal, and how does that internal structure determine the way the metal behaves when it is loaded, heated, or worked?

Week 1 builds the metal from the inside out: atoms and valence electrons explain metallic bonding (why metals conduct and bend instead of shatter); solid solutions show how alloying atoms strain the lattice; unit cells (BCC, FCC, HCP) set how easily planes can slip; and grains and grain boundaries turn single crystals into real, polycrystalline metal.

Week 2 connects that structure to behavior. Stress, strain, and the elastic-plastic transition give the language for how a metal responds to load, and the five key properties (strength, hardness, ductility, toughness, brittleness) describe the result. Crystal defects explain why it responds that way — dislocations make plastic deformation possible, while grain boundaries, alloy atoms, and other dislocations block them (Hall-Petch, work hardening). Diffusion and the three stages of annealing show how heat lets atoms move and restores a worked metal.

Answer: A metal's behavior is decided by its bonding, crystal structure, grains, and defects. Loading moves dislocations; anything that blocks them makes the metal stronger but less ductile; working piles dislocations up; and heat lets atoms diffuse and the structure rebuild. Every later unit applies this idea.

Iron vs. Steel & Allotropy (Ferrite and Austenite) Process

What it is

Pure iron is soft (yield ≈ 130 MPa). Steel is iron with roughly 0.02–2.14% carbon dissolved interstitially. Allotropy is an element's ability to exist in more than one crystal structure: iron is BCC ferrite (α) below 912°C and FCC austenite (γ) above it.

How it works

Carbon atoms distort the iron lattice and block dislocations, raising yield strength from ~250 MPa to over 2,000 MPa depending on carbon, alloying, and heat treatment. Austenite can dissolve far more carbon than ferrite, so heating steel into austenite and controlling how it cools sets the final microstructure.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Allotropy is the foundation of every steel heat treatment, and it explains why a weld HAZ in steel can harden. Above 2.14% C, the material becomes cast iron (Week 5).

Carbon Content & the Central Tradeoff (Low, Medium & High Carbon Steel) Process

What it is

Low carbon (~0.05–0.30% C): soft, ductile, excellent weldability (A36, 1018, 1020). Medium carbon (~0.30–0.60% C): heat-treatable balance of strength and toughness (1045 shafts, gears). High carbon (~0.60–1.0%+ C): very hard, low ductility, difficult to weld (1095 tools, springs).

How it works

More carbon means more lattice distortion: strength and hardness go up while ductility, toughness, and weldability go down. You can't maximize all properties at once.

When you'll apply it

Use it to pick a grade for a job, to predict whether a part can be hardened, and to flag when a weld will need preheat. High-carbon steels "snap, not bend."

AISI/SAE Steel Designation System Process

What it is

A four-digit code: the first two digits identify the steel family/primary alloying elements; the last two digits give carbon content in hundredths of a percent. Common families: 10xx plain carbon, 41xx Cr-Mo, 43xx Ni-Cr-Mo, 51xx Cr, 52100 bearing steel.

How it works

Read 4140 as: "41" = chromium-molybdenum family, "40" = 0.40% carbon. Read 1045 as plain carbon with 0.45% C (not 45%!).

When you'll apply it

A core daily skill — reading drawings, MTRs, and stock tags. The code instantly tells you composition, expected properties, preheat needs, and heat treatment response.

Alloying Elements & Alloy Steels (including HSLA) Concept

What it is

Mn: hardenability and strength; ties up sulfur as MnS. Cr: hardenability, wear, and (≥10.5%) corrosion resistance. Ni: low-temperature toughness; stabilizes austenite. Mo: hardenability, high-temperature strength, resists temper embrittlement. V: grain refinement and hard carbides. Si: deoxidizer, strength in spring/electrical steels. HSLA steels get strength from small Nb/V/Ti additions and fine grains rather than high carbon.

When you'll apply it

Specify an alloy steel only when plain carbon can't deliver: deep hardening of thick sections (4140, 4340), service above ~300–400°C (Cr-Mo), corrosion (stainless), cryogenic toughness (Ni), or a better strength-to-weight ratio (HSLA).

Hardenability & CCT Diagrams Process

What it is

Hardenability is how deeply a steel can be hardened through its cross-section — not the same as hardness. A CCT (Continuous Cooling Transformation) diagram plots temperature vs. log time with cooling curves overlaid to show which microstructure actually forms during real cooling. Ms/Mf are the martensite start and finish temperatures.

How it works

Trace a cooling curve on the CCT for that specific steel: the transformation regions it crosses are the microstructures that form; faster curves bypass pearlite and bainite to form martensite below Ms. Alloying elements (Cr, Ni, Mo, Mn) shift the curves to the right, so martensite forms at slower cooling rates and in thicker sections.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use CCT reasoning to understand a quench specification and to interpret a WPS — "preheat to 200°C" slows the cooling rate so the HAZ avoids brittle martensite.

Steel Microstructures — Pearlite, Bainite & Martensite Concept

What it is

Pearlite: layered ferrite + cementite plates formed by slow cooling — soft, ductile, machinable. Bainite: fine, feathery ferrite + cementite from intermediate cooling — good strength and toughness. Martensite: a hard, brittle, body-centered tetragonal (BCT) structure formed when austenite cools too fast for carbon to diffuse out — up to ~65 HRC but must be tempered.

When you'll apply it

Microstructure governs hardness, strength, and toughness. Knowing which one a process produced lets you predict whether a part will machine easily, resist wear, or crack.

Heat Treatment Processes — Annealing, Normalizing, Quenching & Tempering Process

What it is

Annealing softens and restores ductility. Normalizing refines grain size. Quenching maximizes hardness by forming martensite. Tempering reheats quenched martensite to restore toughness.

How it works

Anneal: heat into austenite, furnace cool very slowly → coarse pearlite. Normalize: heat into austenite, cool in still air → fine pearlite. Quench: heat into austenite, cool rapidly in water, oil, or polymer → martensite (never used as-quenched alone). Temper: reheat martensite to about 150–650°C and air cool → tempered martensite; higher temperature gives more toughness and less hardness.

When you'll apply it

Anneal after cold work or before machining; normalize forgings and castings before further processing; quench and temper shafts, gears, and tools that need strength and toughness. On the job you verify each with a hardness test, and "normalize and temper" on a drawing means two separate heating cycles.

Case Hardening — Carburizing & Nitriding Process

What it is

Case hardening hardens only a part's surface layer, leaving a tough, ductile core. Carburizing diffuses carbon into a low-carbon steel surface; nitriding diffuses nitrogen to form hard nitrides.

How it works

Carburizing: heat a low-carbon part in a carbon-rich atmosphere at high temperature so carbon diffuses into the surface, then quench to form a martensitic case. Nitriding: diffuse nitrogen in at a lower temperature — no quench required, so distortion is minimal.

When you'll apply it

Used for gears, cams, and shafts that need wear resistance plus impact toughness. Case depth is specified on the drawing; verify it by sectioning a coupon, etching with nital, and checking hardness.

Carbon Equivalent, Weldability & PWHT Process

What it is

Weldability is the ease of welding a metal without cracking or extensive preheat. Carbon equivalent: CE = %C + %Mn/6 + (%Cr+%Mo+%V)/5 + (%Ni+%Cu)/15. Post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) relieves stress and tempers the HAZ after welding.

How it works

Plug the chemistry from the MTR into the CE formula (do each division first, then add). CE below ~0.40: generally weldable without preheat; ~0.40–0.60: preheat required; above ~0.60: preheat mandatory and PWHT very likely.

When you'll apply it

Calculate CE before welding any medium-carbon or alloy steel to set preheat and decide on PWHT. High-CE steels are prone to hydrogen cracking in the HAZ (Week 15); never weld alloy steel in the hardened condition without PWHT.

What Makes Stainless Steel "Stainless" — the Passive Layer Process

What it is

All stainless steels contain at least 10.5% chromium. The chromium forms a thin, invisible, self-repairing passive chromium oxide (Cr₂O₃) layer that shields the steel from oxygen and moisture.

How it works

Chromium reacts with oxygen in the air to form the film; if scratched, it reforms on its own as long as oxygen is present. Chlorides, oxygen-poor crevices, or contamination can break the film down, and then corrosion can be rapid. Ni stabilizes austenite, Mo improves chloride pitting resistance, and C allows hardening.

When you'll apply it

Explains why stainless can still corrode (pitting, crevice, SCC) and why fabrication practice matters — anything that damages or contaminates the passive layer defeats the material.

Stainless Steel Families — Austenitic, Ferritic, Martensitic & Duplex Concept

What it is

Austenitic (300 series): FCC, non-magnetic, hardened only by cold work — 304, 316. Ferritic (400 series): BCC, magnetic, not hardenable — 430. Martensitic (400 series): BCC/BCT, magnetic, hardenable by quench and temper — 410, 420, 440C. Duplex: mixed austenite + ferrite, higher strength and better SCC resistance — 2205, 2507.

When you'll apply it

Identify the family to know whether a part can be heat treated, whether it will respond to a magnet (a quick field check), and what welding precautions apply. Austenitic is the workhorse (>70% of all stainless); duplex is common in oil and gas, chemical, and marine service.

304 vs. 316 & Low-Carbon L-Grades Concept

What it is

304 (18% Cr, 8% Ni) is the general-purpose grade. 316 (18% Cr, 10% Ni, 2–3% Mo) adds molybdenum for much better chloride pitting and crevice resistance. L-grades (304L, 316L) have low carbon to prevent sensitization in welded fabrication.

When you'll apply it

Specify 316 for marine, seawater, or chlorine-washdown environments, or when 304 has already failed. Always specify the L-grade for welded assemblies headed for corrosive service.

Sensitization & Intergranular Corrosion Process

What it is

Sensitization is chromium carbide precipitation at grain boundaries when austenitic stainless is held at about 425–815°C, such as during welding. Intergranular corrosion is the resulting attack along those grain boundaries.

How it works

In the sensitizing range, carbon combines with chromium at grain boundaries, pulling nearby chromium below the 10.5% needed for passivity. Those depleted zones lose their protective film and corrode. Prevent it with low-carbon L-grades or stabilized grades (321, 347 with Ti/Nb), and control heat input and interpass temperature.

When you'll apply it

Watch for it in any welded stainless in corrosive service — it appears again in weld HAZs (Week 15) and as a cause in corrosion failure investigations (Week 16).

Cast Iron — Definition & Why Carbon Form Matters Process

What it is

Cast iron is an iron-carbon alloy with more than 2.14% carbon (typically 2.5–4%) plus 1–3% silicon. It's a distinct family, not low-quality steel. The excess carbon precipitates during solidification as either graphite or cementite (Fe₃C).

How it works

Silicon content and cooling control which form appears: more Si favors graphite, less Si favors cementite. The shape of the graphite — flakes, spheroids, or rosettes — then sets the type and properties. Cast iron melts at ~1150–1200°C (vs. ~1500°C for steel), so it pours easily into complex shapes.

When you'll apply it

Knowing the carbon form tells you whether a casting can take impact, be machined, or be welded.

Cast Iron Types — Gray, White, Ductile & Malleable Process

What it is

Gray iron: graphite flakes — excellent damping and machinability, low tensile strength. White iron: cementite — extremely hard, brittle, wear-resistant. Ductile (nodular) iron: Mg/Ce additions make graphite form as spheroids — much tougher, up to ~900 MPa. Malleable iron: white iron annealed so cementite converts to temper carbon rosettes — good ductility and machinability in thin sections.

How it works

Malleable iron is made by a long anneal of white iron, during which cementite decomposes into irregular temper carbon clusters.

When you'll apply it

Choose gray iron for machine bases, engine blocks, and brake rotors (vibration damping); white iron for mill liners and crusher plates (wear); ductile iron for crankshafts, gears, and heavy pipe (strength at lower cost than steel); malleable iron for fittings and hand tools.

Fabrication Considerations for Stainless & Cast Iron Process

What it is

Special shop practices for these families: controlling sensitization, preheating martensitic stainless, welding cast iron, and preventing stainless contamination.

How it works

Martensitic stainless: preheat ~200–300°C and PWHT (temper ~650–750°C); never weld in the hardened condition. Cast iron: CE is usually >1.0, so use heavy preheat (400–600°C for gray iron) or cold welding with nickel filler, then slow cool — the risk is white iron forming in the HAZ. Stainless contamination: use dedicated stainless tools, discs, and brushes; avoid carbon-steel particles and chloride cleaners.

When you'll apply it

Apply these whenever you weld, grind, or repair stainless or cast iron parts, or review a WPS that calls for them. Understanding why each step exists is what separates a technician from someone following a checklist.

Machinability Concept

What it is

Machinability is a material's relative ease of being cut, drilled, or machined to a good surface finish with reasonable forces, low tool wear, and manageable chips.

When you'll apply it

Used when selecting a material for machined parts (gray iron and free-machining brass rank high; austenitic stainless work-hardens and is harder to cut) and when planning cutting parameters in Week 12.

Week 6 Nonferrous Metals & Phase Diagrams

Lecture slides (PDF)

Why Nonferrous Metals Exist Alongside Steel Concept

What it is

Nonferrous metals are metals not based on iron — aluminum, copper, titanium, nickel, and others — used where steel is too heavy, not conductive enough, or not corrosion-resistant enough.

When you'll apply it

Material selection: aluminum where weight matters; copper for conductivity; brass for fittings and machinability; bronze for bearings and wear; titanium when steel is too heavy and stainless isn't corrosion-resistant enough.

Aluminum Alloy Series & Temper Designations Process

What it is

Wrought aluminum uses a 4-digit series by principal alloying element: 1xxx pure Al, 2xxx Cu, 3xxx Mn, 5xxx Mg, 6xxx Mg+Si, 7xxx Zn. 2xxx, 6xxx, and 7xxx are heat-treatable; 1xxx, 3xxx, and 5xxx are strengthened only by work hardening. The temper code after the number gives the actual condition: O annealed, H strain-hardened, T4 solution treated + naturally aged, T6 solution treated + artificially aged, T651 T6 + stress-relieved by stretching.

How it works

Read 6061-T6 as: 6xxx (Mg+Si) series, heat-treatable, in the solution-treated and artificially aged condition — near-peak strength.

When you'll apply it

The temper code determines real strength — 6061-O and 6061-T6 are very different materials. Aluminum's low melting point (~660°C) and tenacious oxide also drive welding choices (GTAW with AC polarity, or GMAW).

Solution Treatment & Aging (Precipitation Hardening) Process

What it is

Solution treatment and aging is a two-step heat treatment for precipitation-hardenable alloys (heat-treatable aluminum, 17-4PH stainless, some copper alloys).

How it works

(1) Solution treat: heat to dissolve alloying elements into a single solid solution, then quench to trap them in a supersaturated state. (2) Age: hold at room temperature (natural aging, T4) or a moderately elevated temperature (artificial aging, T6) so fine strengthening precipitates form and block dislocations.

When you'll apply it

Explains aluminum tempers, why welding can soften a T6 aluminum HAZ (the heat over-ages or dissolves the precipitates), and why some parts must be re-aged after forming or welding.

Copper & Its Alloys — Brass, Bronze & Copper-Nickel Concept

What it is

Brass is copper-zinc; bronze is copper-tin (or copper-aluminum); copper-nickel has ~5–30% Ni and outstanding seawater resistance. Copper alloys are numbered in the UNS Cxxxxx system (developed by the CDA), e.g., C11000 copper, C26000 cartridge brass, C36000 free-machining brass.

When you'll apply it

Specify by function: pure copper for electrical and thermal conductivity, brass for fittings and machined parts, bronze for bearings and wear surfaces, copper-nickel for marine piping and condensers.

Titanium & Its Alloy Families Concept

What it is

Commercially pure (CP) titanium (ASTM Grades 1–4) is essentially all-alpha (HCP) with excellent corrosion resistance and formability. Alloy families are alpha, near-alpha, alpha-beta, and beta. Grade 5 (Ti-6Al-4V) is the workhorse alpha-beta alloy; Grade 23 (Ti-6Al-4V ELI) has extra-low interstitials for higher toughness in implants.

When you'll apply it

Used in aerospace and medical applications where high strength-to-weight and corrosion resistance justify the cost. Titanium requires specialized welding procedures with full inert shielding — temper (oxidation) colors on a Ti weld tell you the shielding was inadequate.

Galvanic Risk with Dissimilar Metals Process

What it is

When two different metals touch in the presence of moisture, the more anodic (active) metal corrodes faster. Aluminum is anodic to steel and copper.

How it works

The dissimilar metals form a corrosion cell: electrons flow from the anodic metal (aluminum) to the cathodic metal (steel/copper) through the contact, and the anodic metal is consumed. Isolation gaskets, sleeves, or washers break the electrical path.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Check any assembly that mixes aluminum with steel or copper fasteners in a wet environment. Covered in depth in Week 16.

The Iron-Carbon Phase Diagram Process

What it is

A phase diagram is a reference chart showing which phases are stable at each composition and temperature under slow (equilibrium) cooling. The iron-carbon diagram maps ferrite, austenite, cementite, and pearlite. The eutectoid point is 0.77% C at 727°C, where austenite transforms to ferrite + cementite (pearlite).

How it works

Find the steel's carbon content on the horizontal axis and the temperature on the vertical axis; the region where they meet is the stable phase. Classify steels from the eutectoid: hypoeutectoid (<0.77% C) cools to ferrite + pearlite; eutectoid (0.77% C) cools to all pearlite; hypereutectoid (>0.77% C) cools to cementite + pearlite. No calculations needed — it's read like a map.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use it to choose heat-treat temperatures (how hot to get fully into austenite), to predict slow-cooled microstructures, and to interpret why a weld HAZ hardens in medium-carbon steel. Unlike the CCT diagram, the phase diagram is universal for iron-carbon, not alloy-specific.

Weight Percent vs. Atomic Percent Concept

What it is

Weight percent (wt%) is each element's share of total mass; atomic percent (at%) is its share of the total number of atoms.

When you'll apply it

Phase diagrams, alloy specs, and MTRs are read in wt% — confirm which basis a value uses before comparing compositions from different sources.

Reading Heat Treatment Specs & Temper Colors Process

What it is

Practical technician knowledge for verifying heat treatment: specifications on drawings, the role of hardness verification, and temper colors — oxide colors that indicate the surface temperature a steel reached.

How it works

Temper colors form as a thin oxide film thickens with temperature: straw ≈ 200°C, purple ≈ 300°C on steel. Along with pyrometers and heat-indicating crayons, they give a quick field estimate of temperature.

When you'll apply it

Use when checking a heat-treated part against its drawing (verify tempering with a hardness test), when inspecting stainless or titanium welds for overheating or poor shielding, and when estimating temperature without an instrument.

Unit 3 driving question

Week 7 Mechanical Testing Methods

Lecture slides (PDF)

Tensile Testing & the Stress-Strain Curve Process

What it is

A tensile test pulls a specimen along its axis at a controlled rate until it necks and fractures, continuously recording load and elongation to find elastic modulus, yield strength, UTS, and ductility.

How it works

Machine a standard specimen, measure its gauge length and area, load it in the test frame, and pull to fracture. Convert load to stress and elongation to strain to plot the curve. When there's no clear yield point (e.g., aluminum), use the 0.2% offset method: draw a line parallel to the elastic slope starting at 0.002 strain; where it crosses the curve is the yield strength. Ductility: %EL = (Lf − L₀)/L₀ × 100 and %RA = (A₀ − Af)/A₀ × 100.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Tensile results appear on every MTR. Use them to confirm a material meets its spec and to compare candidate materials.

Hardness Testing — Rockwell, Brinell & Vickers Process

What it is

Hardness testing presses a standardized indenter into the surface under a known load and converts the impression size or depth into a number. Rockwell: diamond cone or ball, reads depth directly. Brinell: large ball, measures impression diameter. Vickers: diamond pyramid, measures impression diagonals.

How it works

Rockwell: apply a minor load, then a major load, and read the hardness from penetration depth — fast and simple. Brinell: press a hardened ball under heavy load and measure the impression diameter; HB = 2F / [πD(D − √(D² − d²))]. Vickers: press a small diamond pyramid and measure the diagonals — one continuous scale for all hardnesses. Hardness numbers are empirical; convert between scales only with a lookup table.

When you'll apply it

Rockwell is the shop-floor check for finished heat-treated steel parts; Brinell suits castings and coarse-grained materials; Vickers suits thin sections, coatings, and microhardness surveys across a weld/HAZ. Hardness is how you verify heat treatment was done right.

Impact Testing & the Ductile-Brittle Transition Process

What it is

Charpy and Izod tests measure the energy a notched specimen absorbs when struck by a swinging pendulum — a direct measure of toughness. The ductile-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) is where a material's fracture shifts from tough (upper shelf) to brittle (lower shelf).

How it works

Charpy: the specimen lies horizontally on two supports and is struck behind the notch. Izod: it's clamped vertically as a cantilever and struck on the notch side. Energy absorbed is the difference in pendulum height before and after (E = mgh), reported in J or ft·lbf. Testing at several temperatures traces the transition curve.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Critical for parts in cold service — BCC steels can become brittle in the cold, while FCC metals don't. Charpy values that only just meet minimum on a low-temperature project should be flagged to the engineer.

Fatigue Testing — Cyclic Loading & the S-N Curve Process

What it is

Fatigue is failure from repeated cyclic stress below the yield strength. The S-N (Wöhler) curve plots stress amplitude (half the stress range) against cycles to failure on a log scale. The fatigue (endurance) limit is the stress steel can survive indefinitely; the fatigue ratio is fatigue limit ÷ UTS.

How it works

Specimens are cycled at a set stress amplitude until they fail; repeating at different stress levels builds the curve. For steels the curve flattens at the fatigue limit; many nonferrous metals (like aluminum) have no true limit.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Any rotating, vibrating, or pressure-cycled part is a fatigue concern. Use S-N data to understand why surface finish, notches, and weld toes control service life.

Reading a Mill Test Report (MTR) & Material Traceability Process

What it is

A Mill Test Report (MTR) is a certificate that links a heat number or lot number to the material's grade, chemical composition, and mechanical test results. Traceability ties the physical material back to that document.

How it works

Match the heat number stenciled on the material to the MTR, then check the grade, chemistry (use it to calculate CE), and mechanical properties against the specification.

When you'll apply it

Do this at material receiving and before fabrication. If the heat number doesn't match, stop: quarantine the material, raise a nonconformance report, and notify quality — unverified material can't go into a code-governed system.

Week 8 Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) Methods

Lecture slides (PDF)

Visual Testing (VT) Process

What it is

Visual testing is the foundational inspection method — the naked eye aided by proper lighting, magnification, mirrors, and borescopes for internal or hard-to-reach areas.

How it works

Clean the surface, provide adequate lighting (≥ 500 lux or per procedure), and examine for surface defects such as cracks, undercut, overlap, porosity, and corrosion. Use gauges to measure what you see (e.g., weld size).

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Every inspection starts with VT; it's the cheapest method and catches many weld and surface defects. It only finds surface-visible conditions.

Liquid / Dye Penetrant Testing (PT) Process

What it is

Penetrant testing uses capillary action to draw a colored or fluorescent liquid into surface-breaking defects, then makes them visible with a developer.

How it works

(1) Pre-clean the surface. (2) Apply penetrant and wait the required dwell time. (3) Remove excess penetrant from the surface. (4) Apply developer, which draws penetrant back out of defects. (5) Inspect under white light (color contrast) or UV light (fluorescent) and record indications.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use on any non-porous material — including non-magnetic metals like aluminum and austenitic stainless — to find surface-breaking cracks, laps, and seams. It cannot find subsurface defects and fails on dirty or porous surfaces.

Magnetic Particle Testing (MT) Process

What it is

Magnetic particle testing finds surface and near-surface defects in ferromagnetic materials by detecting flux leakage where a defect interrupts a magnetic field.

How it works

Magnetize the part using a yoke (electromagnet straddling the area) or prods (current passed through the part). A defect forces field lines out of the surface; iron particles (dry or in a wet suspension, visible or fluorescent) collect there and outline it. Inspect in at least two perpendicular directions, since defects parallel to the field don't show.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

The go-to method for cracks in carbon and low-alloy steel welds and forgings. It doesn't work on aluminum, copper, or austenitic stainless.

Ultrasonic Testing (UT) Process

What it is

Ultrasonic testing sends high-frequency sound into a part to detect internal defects and measure thickness. A transducer converts electrical pulses to sound; a flaw detector displays echoes as an A-scan (amplitude vs. time/depth). Couplant (gel, oil, water) is needed because air blocks ultrasound. Probes can be straight-beam, angle-beam, or phased array (PAUT).

How it works

Sound travels into the part; any reflector (crack, void, back wall) sends an echo back. Travel time gives distance. For angle-beam UT, slant path L = vS·t/2; depth = L·cos θ; surface distance from the probe index point = L·sin θ, where θ is the refracted angle (45°, 60°, 70°).

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use for internal defects and planar cracks deep in welds and plate, and for wall-thickness measurement on corroded pipe from one side. Required on rolled plate in through-thickness-loaded joints to find laminations.

Radiographic Testing (RT) Process

What it is

Radiographic testing passes X-rays or gamma rays through a part onto film or a digital detector to image volumetric defects such as porosity, inclusions, and voids.

How it works

Denser or thicker material absorbs more radiation, so voids and lower-density inclusions appear darker on the image. It requires access to both sides (source on one side, detector on the other) and strict radiation safety.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Best for porosity and slag in welds and castings, and it produces a permanent record. It can miss planar cracks that run parallel to the beam.

Eddy Current Testing (ET) Process

What it is

Eddy current testing uses an alternating magnetic field to induce circulating currents in a conductive part; cracks and corrosion disturb those currents. Penetration is limited by skin depth: δ = 503/√(f·μr·σ).

How it works

A coil carrying AC current is placed near the surface; it induces eddy currents in the part. A crack or thinning changes the current flow, which changes the coil's impedance and shows on the instrument. Higher frequency, conductivity, or permeability gives shallower penetration.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Used for surface cracks, conductivity sorting, coating thickness, and tube inspection on conductive metals; pulsed eddy current is used to detect corrosion under insulation.

NDT Method Selection & Technician Qualification Process

What it is

Choosing the right NDT method by asking about defect location, material type, defect orientation, access, and whether a permanent record is needed. ASNT SNT-TC-1A defines qualification levels: Level I performs tests under supervision; Level II performs, interprets, and accepts/rejects; Level III writes procedures and certifies.

How it works

Work through the questions: surface only → VT/PT; ferromagnetic surface/near-surface → MT; deep internal → UT or RT; planar cracks → UT preferred; one-side access → not RT; permanent image needed → RT or digital UT.

When you'll apply it

Use whenever an inspection plan is written or a method is questioned. Using the wrong method can miss the defect entirely.

Week 9 Quality Standards & Inspection

Lecture slides (PDF)

Standards Organizations & Codes Process

What it is

ASTM: material specifications and test methods. AWS: welding codes, procedure/welder qualification, filler classification (e.g., D1.1 Structural Welding Code — Steel). ASME: Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC). ISO: international quality and materials standards. API: oil and gas pipeline, drilling, and refinery standards.

How it works

Identify the governing code from the contract or drawing, confirm the edition/revision, and then apply its acceptance criteria — they differ between codes.

When you'll apply it

Before evaluating any indication, confirm which standard applies. A weld that passes AWS D1.1 may fail ASME Section VIII — using the wrong code is as serious as missing a defect.

Common Weld Defects (Manufacturing Discontinuities) Concept

What it is

Porosity: trapped gas pockets. Slag inclusion: trapped flux between passes. Lack of fusion (LOF): weld metal not bonded to base metal or a prior pass. Lack of penetration (LOP): weld doesn't extend through the joint root. Cracks. Undercut: a groove melted into the base metal at the toe. Overlap: weld metal rolled over the base metal without fusing.

When you'll apply it

Recognizing each defect and its cause (contamination, poor slag removal, low heat input, high travel speed, poor joint prep) lets you choose the right NDT method and tell the welder what to correct.

Service-Induced Defects — SCC & Creep Concept

What it is

Defects that develop during use rather than manufacturing. Stress corrosion cracking (SCC): tensile stress + a specific corrosive environment causes cracking below yield. Creep: slow, time-dependent deformation under constant stress at elevated temperature.

When you'll apply it

Relevant when inspecting equipment that has been in service — high-temperature piping, boilers, and stainless vessels in chloride environments.

Indication → Discontinuity → Defect → Reject Process

What it is

The precise evaluation sequence: an indication is any response from an NDT method; a discontinuity is a confirmed interruption in the part's structure; a defect is a discontinuity that exceeds the governing code's acceptance criteria; a defect leads to a reject decision.

How it works

Detect the indication → evaluate whether it's relevant (a real discontinuity, not a false or non-relevant indication) → measure it and compare against the acceptance criteria → if it exceeds them, it's a defect and the part is rejected.

When you'll apply it

Use these words exactly in every report — they have distinct legal meanings, and not every indication is a defect.

Inspection Documentation, Traceability & NCRs Process

What it is

Inspection reports are the legal record of every inspection. Traceability links each record to the physical component through unique identifiers. A Non-Conformance Report (NCR) formally documents a rejected component and its disposition.

How it works

A complete report includes: component ID and heat/lot number, applicable standard and revision, NDT method and procedure number, equipment and calibration date, inspector name and certification level, date/location/conditions, indication details, acceptance criteria applied, disposition, and signature. When a part is rejected, raise an NCR stating the non-conformance, the disposition (repair, rework, scrap, or use-as-is with engineering approval), and re-inspection requirements.

When you'll apply it

Every day on the job. If it isn't documented, it didn't happen — a report missing a procedure number or inspector level shouldn't be accepted, and the records are what make a failure investigation possible.

Hydrostatic Testing Process

What it is

Hydrostatic testing is a pressure proof test in which a vessel or piping system is filled with water and pressurized above its rated working pressure (typically 1.5×) to verify it holds pressure without leaking or failing.

How it works

Fill the system with water, remove air, raise pressure to the code-required test value, hold for the specified time, and inspect for leaks or pressure drop. Water is used because it stores little energy compared to compressed gas.

When you'll apply it

Required by codes such as ASME and API before a pressure component goes into service, and after repairs to pressure-retaining welds.

Unit 4 driving question

How are metal parts made — and how does knowing the process tell me which defects to expect and which quality checks to apply?

Week 10 Casting Processes & Defect Recognition

Lecture slides (PDF)

Solidification & Shrinkage Process

What it is

As molten metal freezes in a mold it forms three zones: a chill zone of fine random grains at the cold mold wall, a columnar zone of long grains growing inward along heat flow, and an equiaxed zone of coarse random grains in the center. Grains grow as tree-like dendrites; finer dendrite arm spacing means better properties. Metal contracts as it solidifies (solidification shrinkage).

How it works

Heat leaves through the mold wall, so freezing starts there and moves inward. A riser (feeder) — a reservoir of liquid metal — is placed so it freezes last and feeds liquid into the casting as it shrinks; without it, internal shrinkage voids form.

When you'll apply it

Explains where casting defects form (in the last areas to freeze, at thick sections) and why faster cooling gives finer, stronger structures.

Sand Casting Process

What it is

Sand casting pours molten metal into a cavity formed in packed sand around a pattern (a slightly oversized replica of the part). Cores form internal passages. The gating system — pouring cup, sprue (vertical channel), runner (horizontal distribution), and gates (entry into the cavity) — delivers the metal.

How it works

Pack sand around the pattern in a two-part flask, remove the pattern, place cores, close the mold, and pour metal through the gating system. After solidification, break away the sand, remove gating and risers, and clean the casting. Each mold is used once.

When you'll apply it

The most flexible process — any alloy, nearly any size, complex internal geometry, low tooling cost — but rough finish (Ra 6–25 μm) and loose tolerances. Choose it for low-to-medium volume and large parts.

Die Casting (Hot Chamber vs. Cold Chamber) Process

What it is

Die casting injects molten metal under high pressure into a reusable, precision-machined steel die. Hot-chamber machines keep the injection system submerged in the melt (zinc, magnesium); cold-chamber machines ladle metal into a separate shot chamber (aluminum, copper).

How it works

Molten metal is forced into the closed die at high velocity, held under pressure while it solidifies, and ejected; the cycle repeats rapidly.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

High volume, tight tolerances, good finish — but limited to lower-melting alloys (Al, Zn, Mg, Cu), never steel or cast iron. Expect fine gas porosity from trapped air; die castings may blister if heat treated.

Investment (Lost-Wax) Casting Process

What it is

Investment casting builds a ceramic shell around a wax pattern, melts the wax out, and pours metal into the shell — the best surface finish and accuracy of any casting process.

How it works

Make wax patterns (often assembled on a tree), dip repeatedly in ceramic slurry and stucco to build a shell, melt/burn out the wax, preheat the shell, pour the metal, then break away the shell.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Choose for small-to-medium precision parts in almost any alloy, including superalloys and stainless — e.g., when a stainless bracket was wrongly specified for die casting.

Casting Defects — Recognition & Detection Concept

What it is

Shrinkage porosity: irregular, jagged voids from inadequate feeding. Gas porosity: smooth, spherical voids from dissolved/trapped gas. Hot tear: cracking while the metal is partly solid, from mold restraint. Cold shut: two metal streams that failed to fuse. Misrun: incomplete filling. Inclusions from sand or slag.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use defect appearance to trace the root cause. A hot tear has oxidized (dark) fracture faces because it formed at high temperature; a cold crack has clean metallic faces. RT finds porosity; PT/MT find surface cracks.

Casting Process Selection Process

What it is

Choosing among sand, die, and investment casting using five factors: alloy, part size, production volume, tolerance and finish, and internal geometry.

How it works

Eliminate processes that can't handle the alloy first (die casting can't do steel), then check size limits, then match volume to tooling cost, then tolerance/finish, then whether cores are needed.

When you'll apply it

Use when reviewing a job order or drawing to catch an incompatible process specification before parts are made.

Week 11 Metal Forming Processes

Lecture slides (PDF)

Hot Working vs. Cold Working Process

What it is

The boundary is the recrystallization temperature (Trex ≈ 0.4 Tm). Hot working above it causes no work hardening; cold working below it work-hardens the metal.

How it works

Above Trex, the metal recrystallizes continuously as it deforms, so large reductions take less force — but the surface scales and dimensions are less precise. Below Trex, dislocations accumulate, raising strength and giving smooth surfaces and tight tolerances, but requiring more force and periodic annealing.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Explains the difference between hot-rolled and cold-rolled stock you'll receive, and when an intermediate anneal is needed during forming.

Work Hardening, Anisotropy & Strain Aging Process

What it is

Work hardening is strengthening from dislocation buildup during cold work, quantified by percent cold work: %CW = (A₀ − Af)/A₀ × 100. Anisotropy is direction-dependent properties from crystallographic texture. Strain aging is interstitial atoms pinning dislocations after cold work, raising yield and causing Lüders bands (stretcher strains).

How it works

As dislocations multiply, they tangle and block each other, so more stress is needed to keep deforming (strength ∝ √dislocation density). Cold working also rotates grains into a preferred orientation, which makes properties differ by direction.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Use %CW to predict strength and ductility of formed parts, and expect different properties along vs. across the rolling direction. Lüders bands are a cosmetic and forming issue in low-carbon sheet.

Rolling (Hot, Cold & Shape Rolling) Process

What it is

Rolling reduces thickness or forms a cross-section by passing metal between rotating rolls — the highest-volume forming process. Hot rolling makes plate and structural shapes; cold rolling gives better finish and tolerance; shape rolling uses grooved rolls to make I-beams, channels, angles, and rails.

How it works

The rolls grip the stock by friction and squeeze it through a gap smaller than its thickness; repeated passes progressively reduce it.

When you'll apply it

Rolled plate can contain laminations lying parallel to the surface that VT, MT, and PT can't see — UT rolled plate before using it in through-thickness-loaded welded joints.

Forging & Grain Flow Process

What it is

Forging shapes metal by compressive hammering or pressing — the highest-integrity wrought parts. Open-die forging uses flat or simple dies for large, simple shapes; closed-die (impression-die) forging fills a matched cavity. Grain flow is the alignment of grains along the part's contours; flash is excess metal squeezed out at the parting line.

How it works

Heat a billet (usually hot forging), place it between dies, and compress it so metal flows to fill the shape; trim the flash in closed-die forging. Draft angles let the part release from the die.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Specify forgings for crankshafts, connecting rods, and other fatigue-critical parts — aligned grain flow improves fatigue life. When evaluating a crack in a forging, check its orientation relative to grain flow.

Extrusion Process

What it is

Extrusion forces metal through a die opening to produce long, constant-cross-section profiles. In direct extrusion the ram and billet move together toward the die; in indirect extrusion the die moves into a stationary billet.

How it works

A heated billet sits in a container and a ram pushes it through the die, like toothpaste from a tube; the profile takes the shape of the die opening.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Common for aluminum (6xxx) frames, rails, and tubing. Watch for center-burst (chevron) cracking and pipe defects carried in from the billet.

Drawing (Wire, Tube & Deep Drawing) Process

What it is

Drawing pulls metal through a die under tension to reduce its cross-section. Wire drawing uses progressively smaller dies; tube drawing controls the bore by sinking (no tool), fixed plug, floating plug, or mandrel. Deep drawing uses a punch to pull a flat blank into a die to make a cup-shaped part.

How it works

The material is pulled, not pushed, so each pass is limited by the strength of the drawn product. Too much reduction creates hydrostatic tension in the deformation zone that can open internal voids.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Used for wire, hydraulic tubing, and cans/cups. Linear indications parallel to the axis on drawn pressure tubing (seams or laps) are rejected, not sized.

Sheet Metal Operations & Forming Limits Concept

What it is

Sheet metal processes — shearing, bending, stamping, and deep drawing — shape thin sheet. Forming limit diagrams map the strain combinations a sheet can survive; plane strain is the most severe point. Wrinkling occurs under compressive strains; necking and fracture occur under stretching.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use when inspecting stamped and drawn parts to tell whether a failure came from too much stretch (necking/splitting) or too much compression (wrinkling) — and to know which way to adjust the process.

Forming Defects Concept

What it is

Laps: metal folds over itself without fusing. Seams: elongated surface defects from flaws in the original stock. Bursts: internal or surface cracks where ductility was exceeded. Pipe: an ingot's shrinkage cavity drawn out into a long internal void. Chevron (center-burst) cracking: V-shaped internal cracks along the centerline of drawn or extruded bar.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Knowing the process tells you where to look: PT/MT for surface laps and seams; UT for internal bursts, pipe, and laminations.

Week 12 Machining

Lecture slides (PDF)

The Cutting Zone & Chip Formation Process

What it is

Machining removes metal by shearing it off as a chip. Deformation happens in the primary zone (the shear plane, where the chip forms), the secondary zone (friction along the tool face), and the tertiary zone (the tool flank rubbing the new surface). Chips are continuous (ductile metals) or discontinuous (brittle metals); a built-up edge (BUE) is workpiece metal welded to the tool.

How it works

The tool's edge compresses the metal ahead of it until it shears along the shear plane and slides up the tool face as a chip. The rake angle controls this: positive rake lowers cutting force; negative rake strengthens the edge for hard or interrupted cuts.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Chip shape and color tell you whether parameters are right. BUE at low cutting speed causes a rough finish — raise the speed or change the tool.

Cutting Parameters — Speed, Feed, Depth of Cut & Chip Load Process

What it is

Cutting speed (Vc): the speed of the cutting edge relative to the work, in m/min. Feed (f): tool advance per revolution or per tooth. Depth of cut (ap): thickness of material removed per pass. Chip load: feed per tooth in milling.

How it works

Speed is set mainly by tool and workpiece material; feed and tool nose radius set the surface finish (Ra ≈ f²/8r); depth of cut and feed set material removal rate and force. Too small a chip load causes rubbing instead of cutting.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Cutting parameters are quality parameters, not just productivity settings — use them to diagnose poor finish, chatter, and fast tool wear.

Turning & Milling Process

What it is

Turning: a single-point tool removes material from a rotating workpiece on a lathe to make cylindrical surfaces. Milling: a rotating multi-tooth cutter removes material from the work to make flats, slots, pockets, and contours.

How it works

In up (conventional) milling, the chip starts thin and gets thicker; in down (climb) milling, it starts thick and gets thinner, usually giving a better finish and lower tensile residual stress (but requiring a rigid, backlash-free machine). Ball-nose milling leaves scallops between passes.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Use to understand how CNC turning and milling centers produce features, and what to measure and document after machining.

Drilling & Grinding Process

What it is

Drilling feeds a rotating drill axially to make round holes; features like countersinks are added after. Grinding uses a rotating abrasive wheel to remove very thin layers, producing the finest finishes and tightest tolerances (Ra ~0.025–0.8 μm), often as the final step on hardened parts.

How it works

In grinding, thousands of abrasive grains each take a tiny chip. Much of the energy becomes heat, so coolant and a sharp (dressed) wheel are essential.

When you'll apply it

Grinding is how hardened shafts and bearing journals reach final size — and it's also where grinding burn can damage them (see machining defects).

Cutting Tool Materials & Tool Wear Process

What it is

From toughest to hardest: HSS (high-speed steel — tough, inexpensive), carbide (WC in cobalt — the modern standard), ceramic (very hard, brittle, high-speed finishing), CBN (hardened steels and superalloys), and PCD (polycrystalline diamond — nonferrous metals and composites only, since diamond reacts with iron). Flank wear on the tool face behind the edge is the main measure of tool life.

How it works

Tool life follows the Taylor equation, Vc·Tⁿ = C — higher cutting speed shortens tool life sharply. Monitor flank wear and replace tools at the specified limit.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use to select a tool for a material and to recognize when a worn tool is degrading surface finish and dimensions.

Surface Finish & Surface Integrity Process

What it is

Surface roughness is measured as Ra (arithmetic mean roughness) and Rz (mean peak-to-valley height). Surface integrity goes beyond roughness to include residual stress, microstructural changes, and hidden damage.

How it works

Measure with a profilometer across the lay of the surface and compare against the drawing callout. Then look at the surface itself — patterns like chatter can be harmful even when Ra is in tolerance.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Surface finish and residual stress control fatigue life. Don't accept a fatigue-critical surface on Ra alone.

Machining Defects & Stress Risers Process

What it is

Grinding burn: thermal damage that re-tempers (softens) or re-hardens the surface. Chatter marks: vibration-induced waves. Built-up edge roughness. EDM recast layer: re-solidified surface with tensile residual stress. Geometric stress risers such as keyways, thread roots, and sharp corners concentrate stress.

How it works

Detect grinding burn with a Nital etch (dilute nitric acid in alcohol), which stains re-tempered and re-hardened zones differently from the base metal — MT can't see it. Trace chatter to tool overhang, fixture rigidity, or parameters, correct it, and re-inspect.

When you'll apply it

Apply to any ground or machined fatigue-critical part (shafts, journals, bores). A dimensionally perfect shaft with grinding burn must still be rejected.

Lecture slides (PDF)

Powder Production — Atomization Process

What it is

Atomization breaks a stream of molten metal into fine droplets that solidify into powder. Water atomization gives irregular particles; gas atomization gives spherical particles.

How it works

A high-pressure jet of water or inert gas hits the falling molten stream and shatters it; droplets freeze in flight and are collected and sieved by size.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Particle shape matters: irregular powders press well into strong green compacts; spherical powders flow and pack well for MIM and additive manufacturing.

Compaction & the Green Compact Process

What it is

Compaction presses powder in a die to form a green compact — a fragile, pressed-but-unsintered part whose handling strength is its green strength. Cold isostatic pressing (CIP) applies equal pressure from all directions at room temperature.

How it works

Fill the die cavity with powder (often mixed with lubricant), press with upper and lower punches, and eject the compact vertically. Friction against the die walls makes density uneven, which CIP avoids for complex shapes.

When you'll apply it

Explains PM density limits and why part geometry is restricted to shapes that can be ejected vertically.

Sintering (Solid-State & Liquid Phase) Process

What it is

Sintering densifies and strengthens a compact by solid-state atomic diffusion below the melting point — not by melting. In liquid phase sintering, a lower-melting additive melts and fills the pores (key to WC-Co cemented carbide tools).

How it works

Heat the green compact in a protective atmosphere: first the lubricant burns off, then atoms diffuse across particle contacts, forming necks that grow and close pores; the part shrinks and gains strength. Some porosity typically remains.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use to understand the properties of sintered parts you'll inspect — and why controlling time, temperature, and atmosphere matters.

Secondary Operations & PM Applications Process

What it is

Operations after sintering — sizing/coining, machining, heat treatment, and oil impregnation — and the typical sintered parts a technician encounters.

How it works

Residual porosity can be filled with oil to make self-lubricating bearings, or the part can be re-pressed to improve density and tolerance.

When you'll apply it

Recognize common PM parts — self-lubricating bushings, gears, structural parts, and carbide cutting tools — and know that their porosity affects strength and inspection.

Advanced PM — MIM & HIP Process

What it is

Metal injection molding (MIM) mixes fine powder with a polymer binder, injection-molds it like plastic, removes the binder (debinding), and sinters. Hot isostatic pressing (HIP) applies heat and high isostatic gas pressure together to eliminate porosity and approach wrought properties.

How it works

MIM: mix feedstock → injection mold → debind thermally or with solvent → sinter (with significant shrinkage). HIP: seal the part or powder in a vessel, apply high-pressure inert gas at high temperature so internal pores collapse and bond.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

MIM for small, complex, high-volume parts; HIP for critical castings, additive parts, and PM parts where porosity can't be tolerated.

Additive Manufacturing (SLM, EBM & DED) Process

What it is

Additive manufacturing builds metal parts layer-by-layer from a 3D model with no tooling. SLM uses a laser to melt a powder bed; EBM uses an electron beam in vacuum with a preheated bed; DED feeds powder or wire into a focused energy source and can add material onto existing parts.

How it works

Spread a thin layer of powder, melt the cross-section with the beam, lower the platform, and repeat (powder bed). DED deposits material directly where the beam melts it.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Used for complex, low-volume parts and repairs. Technicians check for porosity, lack of fusion between layers, residual stress, and dimensional accuracy — often followed by HIP and machining.

PM Defects — Recognition & Detection Concept

What it is

Density gradients: uneven density from uneven fill or pressure. Lamination cracks: planar cracks perpendicular to pressing direction from trapped air or spring-back on ejection. Blistering: surface bubbles when lubricant/binder burns off too fast during sintering. Porosity.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Trace each defect to its process step (fill, pressing, ejection, sintering) so the right step gets corrected.

Manufacturing Process Selection (Unit 4 Summary) Process

What it is

A 5-step framework for choosing between casting, forming, machining, and powder metallurgy: alloy compatibility, part geometry, tolerance & surface finish, production volume, and cost target.

How it works

Work through the five steps in order. Casting: flexible alloys and complex geometry, key defect is porosity. Forming: wrought metals, superior grain flow and fatigue life. Machining: any alloy and geometry, finest finish, but 50–90% material waste. PM: near-net shape, medium-to-high volume, limited alloys.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use when reviewing a drawing or process plan to understand why a process was chosen and which quality checks it requires.

Lecture slides (PDF)

Welding Fundamentals — Joint Types & Groove Preparations Process

What it is

Welding melts base metal (and usually filler) into a weld pool that solidifies into a continuous metallurgical bond. The five joint types are butt, T, lap, corner, and edge. Groove preparations include square, V, U, and bevel. A fillet weld fills a corner with a triangular cross-section, sized by its leg size; the weld root and weld face are its bottom and exposed surface. Welding positions are flat (1), horizontal (2), vertical (3), and overhead (4).

How it works

A heat source melts the joint; as the pool solidifies, a continuous metallic connection forms — the joint isn't glued, it's a continuation of the metal. Groove prep gives access for full penetration on thicker material.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Read joint and groove callouts on drawings and verify fillet size with a weld gauge (throat ≈ 0.707 × leg). Procedure and welder qualifications are position-specific.

Heat Input Process

What it is

Heat input is the arc energy delivered per unit length of weld: HI = (V × A × 60) / (1000 × travel speed in mm/min), in kJ/mm.

How it works

Multiply voltage by amperage (watts), convert to per-minute energy, and divide by travel speed. Higher heat input → slower cooling, coarser grains, wider HAZ, more distortion. Lower heat input → faster cooling, finer grains, but greater hydrogen-cracking risk in hardenable steels.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Heat input limits are written into the WPS; calculate it to verify a welder is within the qualified range.

SMAW — Shielded Metal Arc (Stick) Welding Process

What it is

SMAW uses a consumable electrode coated in flux.

How it works

The arc melts the core wire and coating; the flux forms shielding gas and a slag layer that protects the pool and slows cooling. Slag is removed between passes. Equipment is simple and portable.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Field and repair welding, structural steel, and outdoor work. Low-hydrogen electrodes like E7018 must be kept in a heated rod oven — exposed rods absorb moisture and cause hydrogen cracking.

GMAW — Gas Metal Arc (MIG) Welding Process

What it is

GMAW feeds a continuous bare wire through the gun with external shielding gas (argon, CO₂, or blends). Metal transfer modes are short circuit, spray, and pulsed.

How it works

Short circuit: low heat; the wire touches the pool repeatedly — good for thin material and out of position. Spray: high current; a fine stream of droplets — high deposition, flat/horizontal. Pulsed: current pulses release one droplet per pulse — controlled heat for all positions.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Production welding of steel, stainless, and aluminum. Wind can blow away shielding gas, causing porosity.

GTAW — Gas Tungsten Arc (TIG) Welding Process

What it is

GTAW uses a non-consumable tungsten electrode and inert gas shielding, with filler added separately by hand — the highest-quality arc process.

How it works

The arc between the tungsten and the work melts the base metal while the welder feeds filler rod. DCEN polarity (electrode negative) concentrates heat in the work for steel; AC is used on aluminum to break up the surface oxide.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Thin material, root passes, stainless, titanium, aluminum, and critical welds. Also used without filler for TIG dressing of weld toes (Week 15).

FCAW & SAW Process

What it is

FCAW (flux-cored arc welding) uses a tubular wire filled with flux — self-shielded (FCAW-S) or gas-shielded (FCAW-G). SAW (submerged arc welding) feeds bare wire under a blanket of granular flux.

How it works

FCAW works like GMAW but the flux core produces shielding and slag. In SAW, the arc runs hidden under the flux, giving very high deposition and deep penetration.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

FCAW-S for outdoor structural work where wind defeats gas shielding; SAW for long, straight, thick-section welds such as beams, pipe, and pressure vessels.

Filler Metals — AWS Classification & Selection Process

What it is

The AWS filler metal classification identifies form and properties. E7018: E = electrode, 70 = 70 ksi minimum tensile, 1 = all positions, 8 = low-hydrogen flux. ER308L: electrode/rod, 308 austenitic stainless, L = low carbon. ER4043: aluminum-silicon wire for 6xxx alloys. Dilution is the fraction of melted base metal mixed into the weld.

How it works

Select filler to match or overmatch base metal strength and be chemically compatible; the WPS accounts for dilution (SMAW ~20–30%, GMAW ~25–35%, SAW ~50–70%).

When you'll apply it

Use to confirm the correct consumable is in use before welding starts — wrong filler is a nonconformance.

Preheat & Interpass Temperature Process

What it is

Preheat is the minimum temperature of the base metal before welding; interpass temperature is the temperature maintained between passes. Both slow cooling to prevent HAZ cracking.

How it works

Calculate CE to find the preheat band (<0.40 often none; 0.40–0.60 preheat; >0.60 preheat plus likely PWHT), then follow the WPS/code table. Heat the joint and verify with a temperature-indicating crayon (e.g., Tempilstik) or contact pyrometer immediately before striking the arc, and keep it above minimum through every pass.

When you'll apply it

Required on medium-carbon, alloy, and thick-section steels. A preheat reading taken 30 minutes earlier is not valid — re-heat and re-verify.

Weld Symbols & Welding Procedures (AWS A2.4, WPS) Process

What it is

The weld symbol (AWS A2.4) uses a reference line, arrow, and tail to specify weld type, size, length, pitch, and location. A Welding Procedure Specification (WPS) documents the qualified process, filler, preheat, and parameters. Prequalified joints under codes like AWS D1.1 don't need separate procedure testing.

How it works

Symbol below the reference line = arrow side; above = other side. Size goes to the left of the symbol; length and pitch go to the right (e.g., 6 ▲ 50-200). The tail holds process or specification notes.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Read the symbol to know what to inspect, then measure the finished weld against it. A 5 mm fillet where 6 mm is called out is undersize — raise an NCR.

Week 15 Weld Metallurgy & Failure

Lecture slides (PDF)

Weld Zones & Weld Metal Microstructure Concept

What it is

A weld has a fusion zone (melted and resolidified weld metal), the heat-affected zone (HAZ), and unaffected base metal. Desirable weld metal is fine, interlocking acicular ferrite; allotriomorphic (grain-boundary) ferrite is crack-prone. In stainless welds, the Ferrite Number (FN) (target ~4–8) balances hot-cracking resistance against embrittlement.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Explains why weld metal and HAZ properties differ from the base metal, and why filler chemistry and heat input are controlled.

Heat-Affected Zone Sub-Zones Process

What it is

The HAZ is base metal heated above ~730°C but not melted — the most failure-prone region of a weld. Its four sub-zones by peak temperature are CGHAZ (coarse-grained), FGHAZ (fine-grained), ICHAZ (intercritical), and SCHAZ (subcritical).

How it works

Closest to the fusion line, the CGHAZ is heated far into austenite and grains grow coarse — highest hardness and lowest toughness. Moving outward, peak temperatures drop: the FGHAZ recrystallizes to fine grains, the ICHAZ partially transforms, and the SCHAZ is only tempered.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Hardness surveys across the HAZ verify PWHT; HIC typically appears in the CGHAZ at weld toes.

Hot Cracking & Lamellar Tearing Process

What it is

Hot (solidification) cracking occurs as the weld solidifies, from low-melting films at grain boundaries. Lamellar tearing is step-like, through-thickness cracking in the base plate from elongated MnS inclusions under weld shrinkage stress.

How it works

Hot cracking: sulfur forms low-melting iron sulfide films that tear apart under shrinkage; enough manganese (a good Mn:S ratio) ties sulfur up as MnS instead. Lamellar tearing: shrinkage pulls through the plate thickness and separates it along planar inclusions; prevent it with Z-quality steel and joint design.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Consider for high-restraint T-joints and corner joints in thick plate, and when choosing filler and base metal chemistry.

Hydrogen-Induced Cracking (HIC) / Cold Cracking Process

What it is

HIC is delayed cracking that requires three conditions at once: a susceptible (hard) microstructure, hydrogen, and tensile stress.

How it works

Hydrogen from moisture in flux, contamination, or the air enters the weld pool, diffuses into the hard HAZ, and collects at defects, cracking the metal hours or days after welding. Remove any one condition to prevent it: low-hydrogen consumables kept dry, preheat and interpass control to slow cooling and let hydrogen escape, and PWHT.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Critical on high-CE steels. Because cracking is delayed, AWS D1.1 requires waiting at least 48 hours before final MT on high-CE joints — a weld that passes VT right after welding can still crack later.

Fracture Types — Ductile & Brittle Concept

What it is

Ductile fracture shows visible plastic deformation, a dimpled surface from microvoid coalescence, and often a cup-and-cone shape. Brittle fracture is sudden, with little or no deformation, a flat crystalline surface, and chevron or river marks pointing back to the origin.

Diagrams
When you'll apply it

Reading the fracture surface is the first step in any failure investigation — it tells you whether the part was overloaded, too cold, or contained a crack.

Fatigue Fracture & Fatigue Improvement Process

What it is

Fatigue fracture grows progressively under cyclic loading. Beach marks are visible to the eye; striations require an SEM; ratchet marks show multiple origins. The stress concentration factor (Kt) describes how much a notch amplifies stress.

How it works

A crack initiates at a stress concentration (weld toe, undercut, keyway, thread root), grows a small amount each cycle in Stage II (Paris' Law, da/dN = C(ΔK)ᵐ), then the remaining section fails suddenly. Improve fatigue life by reducing stress concentration (toe grinding, TIG dressing) or adding compressive residual stress (shot peening).

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Use on rotating, vibrating, or cyclically loaded parts like crane booms and shafts — a code-compliant weld toe can still start a fatigue crack.

Failure Analysis Methodology Process

What it is

A systematic process for finding the root cause of a component failure.

How it works

Preserve the fracture surfaces (don't fit pieces back together or clean them) → document with photos and service history → examine visually and under magnification → NDT → mechanical and metallographic testing → identify the root cause → report.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Whenever a part fails. What the technician collects and preserves first determines whether the investigation can be defended — a damaged fracture surface is a dead end.

Week 16 Corrosion & Course Wrap-Up

Lecture slides (PDF)

The Electrochemical Corrosion Cell & Passivation Process

What it is

Corrosion is an electrochemical reaction that needs four elements: an anode (where metal oxidizes and is lost), a cathode (where reduction occurs), an electrolyte (moisture, water, soil), and a metallic path connecting them. Passivation is the formation of a stable, adherent oxide that slows corrosion (stainless, aluminum, titanium).

How it works

Metal atoms at the anode give up electrons and become ions; electrons travel through the metallic path to the cathode, where they're consumed; ions move through the electrolyte to complete the circuit. Remove any one of the four elements and corrosion stops.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

The basis for every prevention method — coatings remove the electrolyte, isolation breaks the metallic path, and cathodic protection changes which metal is the anode.

Uniform Corrosion & Corrosion Allowance Process

What it is

Uniform (general) corrosion is even, predictable surface loss measured in mm/year or mils per year (MPY). A corrosion allowance is extra thickness designed in to accommodate expected loss over the service life.

How it works

Measure remaining wall thickness over time (usually by UT) and divide the loss by the time in service to get the corrosion rate; compare remaining thickness to the minimum required.

When you'll apply it

Used to plan inspection intervals and estimate remaining life of piping and tanks.

Galvanic Corrosion & the Galvanic Series Process

What it is

The galvanic series ranks metals by electrochemical potential in a given environment. Galvanic corrosion is accelerated attack on the less-noble (anodic) metal when it's electrically coupled to a more-noble one in an electrolyte. The area effect: a small anode coupled to a large cathode corrodes fastest.

How it works

The farther apart two metals are in the series, the stronger the driving force. Prevent it by isolating the metals with non-conducting gaskets and sleeves, choosing metals close together in the series, or making the anodic metal's area much larger.

Diagram
When you'll apply it

Check any design that mixes metals — aluminum with steel, carbon-steel tubesheets with stainless tubes, or stainless fasteners in aluminum.

Pitting & Crevice Corrosion (PREN) Process

What it is

Pitting is localized breakdown of a passive film that forms deep pits. The Pitting Resistance Equivalent Number, PREN = %Cr + 3.3×%Mo + 16×%N, ranks an alloy's resistance. Crevice corrosion is localized attack in stagnant, oxygen-depleted gaps under gaskets, in lap joints, or between tubes and tubesheets.

How it works

In a crevice, oxygen is used up and can't be replenished; the trapped solution becomes acidic and concentrates chlorides, so the passive film breaks down inside the gap while the outside stays passive. Pits grow the same way once started.

When you'll apply it

Use PREN to choose a stainless grade for chloride service (316 over 304, duplex over 316). Eliminate crevices in design with butt joints, continuous welds, and seal welds.

Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC) Process

What it is

SCC is cracking caused by tensile stress combined with a specific corrosive environment, occurring below the yield strength — e.g., austenitic stainless in hot chloride service.

How it works

Tensile stress (often residual stress from welding or forming) and the environment work together: the crack tip corrodes, stress opens it further, and fine, branching cracks spread with no plastic deformation and no beach marks (which distinguishes SCC from fatigue).

When you'll apply it

Watch for SCC on stainless vessels and piping under chloride-containing insulation. Remedies include stress relief, shot peening weld toes, chloride-free insulation, and upgrading to duplex stainless.

Protective Coatings — Galvanizing, Thermal Spray, Anodizing, Plating & Paint Process

What it is

Barrier and sacrificial coatings that keep the electrolyte off the metal. Hot-dip galvanizing: zinc metallurgically bonded to steel. Thermal spray: molten or semi-molten droplets sprayed onto a prepared surface. Anodizing: electrochemically thickened oxide on aluminum. Also electroplating, painting, and powder coating.

How it works

Galvanizing: clean the steel and dip it in molten zinc; zinc protects as a barrier and sacrificially. Thermal spray: heat the feedstock and propel it onto a grit-blasted surface. Anodizing: make the aluminum part the anode in an electrolytic bath so its oxide grows thicker and harder.

When you'll apply it

Inspect coatings for thickness (in mils), adhesion, and coverage. Surface preparation determines coating life.

Cathodic Protection Process

What it is

Cathodic protection makes the protected structure the cathode so corrosion happens only at an anode. Sacrificial anodes (zinc, magnesium, aluminum) corrode in place of the structure; impressed current uses a DC power source and inert anodes.

How it works

Connect a more-active metal to the structure, or drive current from an external supply, so the structure's potential is pushed to the protected range. Verify by measuring potential against a Cu/CuSO₄ reference electrode (e.g., the −0.85 V criterion).

When you'll apply it

Used on buried pipelines, ship hulls, tanks, and heat-exchanger tubesheets. Technicians take potential readings and replace depleted anodes.

Design, Material Selection & Corrosion Inspection Process

What it is

Preventing corrosion through design and material choice, and finding it through inspection — including corrosion under insulation (CUI), hidden corrosion beneath insulation.

How it works

Design: choose an inherently resistant alloy, use butt instead of lap joints, slope surfaces to drain, isolate dissimilar metals, and control residual stress (PWHT, shot peening). Inspection: VT for staining, blistering, and pitting; UT thickness for remaining wall; pulsed eddy current or radiographic profiling for CUI.

When you'll apply it

Material selection is the most permanent defense. Use these checks on routine equipment inspections and when investigating a corrosion failure — many real failures involve several mechanisms at once.

Units of Measurement & Dimensional Analysis Process

What it is

The seven SI base units (m, kg, s, K, A, mol, cd) and the derived units built from them — newton (N = kg·m/s²), pascal (Pa = N/m²), joule (J = N·m), watt (W = J/s), hertz (Hz = 1/s). U.S. customary units still common on the shop floor: ksi/psi, ft·lbf, °F, inches and mils, kgf.

How it works

Write the defining equation, substitute the units for each variable, and simplify — if the result doesn't match the expected unit, the formula or an input is wrong. Convert with factors equal to 1 (e.g., 25.4 mm/1 in; 1 ksi = 6.895 MPa; °C = (°F − 32)/1.8).

When you'll apply it

Every calculation in the course. Check the unit before the magnitude — a "150" could be °F, HB, or MPa. Hardness numbers are empirical and need conversion tables, not algebra.

Quick Math Review Process

What it is

Order of operations (PEMDAS), fractions and ratios, percentages, exponents, square roots, scientific notation and log scales, and rearranging equations to solve for an unknown.

How it works

Evaluate innermost parentheses first; do divisions within each term before adding (e.g., the CE formula). Use percentages as whole numbers in CE and PREN, but as decimals for strain. Undo exponents with reciprocal powers (Taylor tool life), undo square roots by squaring (Hall-Petch). Check every answer by substituting it back in.

When you'll apply it

Whenever you use a course formula — CE, Brinell hardness, heat input, Hall-Petch, Taylor tool life, %CW, or Ra — and when reading log-scale charts like S-N curves.

Geometry & Right-Triangle Trigonometry Process

What it is

Basic geometry (angles, circumference, area of circles, rectangles, triangles), the Pythagorean theorem, and SOH-CAH-TOA: sin θ = opposite/hypotenuse, cos θ = adjacent/hypotenuse, tan θ = opposite/adjacent.

How it works

Label the right triangle relative to the known angle, pick the ratio that links the known and unknown sides, and solve (or use inverse trig to find an angle). Keep the calculator in DEG mode.

When you'll apply it

Fillet weld throat (0.707 × leg = cos 45° × leg), angle-beam UT flaw depth and surface distance, Brinell indentation depth, cross-sectional area for stress, draft angles, tool included angles, and countersink angles.

Engineering Equations & Designation Systems Concept

What it is

The collected formulas used across the semester — stress/strain, Hooke's Law, Hollomon, Hall-Petch, %EL/%RA/%CW, Kt and notch sensitivity, Paris' Law, hardness, impact energy, machining (Ra, Taylor, specific cutting energy), heat input, CE, PREN, and eddy current skin depth — plus the designation systems for steel (AISI/SAE), aluminum (series and tempers), copper (UNS Cxxxxx), and titanium (ASTM grades).

When you'll apply it

Reference these whenever you decode a material callout, check a calculation, or prepare for a unit quiz — the Key Terms & Vocabulary Guide PDF contains the full list with worked examples.
