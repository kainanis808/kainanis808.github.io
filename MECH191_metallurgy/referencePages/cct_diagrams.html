Continuous Cooling Transformation (CCT) Diagrams

How cooling rate turns austenite into pearlite, bainite, or martensite

Adapted from ASM International Metals Handbook, Vol. 4 — Heat Treating; DoITPoMS (University of Cambridge) Phase Transformations & TTT/CCT Diagrams TLP; Moniz, Metallurgy, Ch. 14 (Heat Treatment of Steels). Diagram below is a schematic, generic-steel illustration — actual curve shapes, temperatures, and times vary by alloy composition and grain size and should be read from the CCT diagram for the specific steel being heat treated.

A continuous cooling transformation (CCT) diagram shows what microstructure a steel forms as it is cooled continuously from the austenitizing temperature down to room temperature — as a function of cooling rate. It answers the practical heat-treating question: if I furnace-cool, air-cool, oil-quench, or water-quench this part, what will I end up with? This is the companion diagram to the iron-carbon phase diagram — the phase diagram tells you what phases are stable at equilibrium (very slow, infinite-time cooling); the CCT diagram tells you what actually forms at the cooling rates used in the shop, where there usually isn't time for equilibrium to be reached.

CCT vs. TTT Diagrams

Steel textbooks usually introduce transformation diagrams as TTT (time-temperature-transformation, or "isothermal transformation") diagrams first, because they're simpler to construct: a small sample is quenched almost instantly to a fixed sub-critical temperature and held there, and the start/finish times of transformation at that one temperature are measured. A CCT diagram instead uses samples cooled continuously at controlled rates, so it reflects how parts are actually processed.

Rule of thumb: for the same steel, the CCT curves sit below and to the right of the equivalent TTT curves — continuous cooling takes longer to trigger transformation than an instant quench to a fixed temperature. In many plain-carbon steels the pearlite reaction on a CCT diagram also "cuts off" the bainite bay, which is why plain-carbon steels are hard to bainite through continuous cooling alone (isothermal austempering is normally used instead) — alloy steels open the bainite bay back up (see Hardenability below).

Anatomy of a CCT Diagram

Temperature runs down the vertical axis (starting from the austenitizing temperature) and elapsed time runs along the horizontal axis on a log scale — transformations that take a fraction of a second and ones that take hours have to fit on the same plot. A family of cooling curves is drawn over the diagram, one for each cooling method of interest; wherever a cooling curve crosses a transformation region, that microstructure forms.

Key Features

FeatureWhat It Marks
A1 / A3Lower/upper critical temperatures — above these, the steel is fully austenite; transformation on cooling cannot start above A1.
Ps / PfPearlite start / finish curves — the "nose" is the shortest time at which pearlite can begin forming, usually near 500–600°C.
Bs / BfBainite start / finish curves — sit at lower temperature and (in alloy steels) longer time than the pearlite nose.
Ms / MfMartensite start / finish — not curves but flat temperatures; martensite forms by a diffusionless, athermal shear transformation the instant untransformed austenite drops below Ms, regardless of how long it takes to get there.
Critical cooling rateThe fastest cooling curve that just clears (misses) the nose of the C-curve. Any cooling rate at or above this yields 100% martensite; anything slower lets some pearlite or bainite form first.

This schematic is generic — for a real alloy, always read the nose position, Ms/Mf temperatures, and bainite bay shape from that steel's own CCT diagram (found in the ASM Atlas of CCT Diagrams or a mill's data sheet).

Cooling Methods & What They Produce

The four cooling curves in the diagram above correspond to the four heat-treating operations a technician actually performs:

Cooling Method Relative Rate CCT Region Hit Resulting Microstructure Relative Hardness
Furnace cool Anneal Slowest Crosses Ps/Pf near A1 (high temperature) Coarse pearlite Lowest — softest, most machinable/ductile
Air cool Normalize Moderate Crosses Ps/Pf nearer the nose Fine pearlite Moderate — refined grain, higher strength than annealed
Oil quench Fast Clips the bainite bay before reaching Ms Martensite + some bainite (varies with section size) High
Water / brine quench Fastest Misses the nose entirely Martensite (as-quenched) Highest — but brittle until tempered
As-quenched martensite is never used in service as-is. It is hard but brittle, and carries high residual stress. Parts are always tempered — reheated to an intermediate temperature (e.g., ~300–600°C) — after quenching to trade some hardness for toughness. Tempering doesn't change which cooling curve you followed; it's a separate, subsequent diffusion step. See the hardness scales reference for typical quenched-and-tempered hardness values (e.g., 1045 and 4140 Q&T @ 315°C).

Hardenability: Why Alloy Steels Behave Differently

Hardenability is how deep and how easily a steel can be hardened (turned to martensite) — it is not the same thing as maximum hardness (which mostly depends on carbon content). Alloying elements such as manganese, chromium, molybdenum, and nickel don't make martensite any harder; they slow down the diffusional pearlite/bainite reactions, which pushes the nose of the C-curve to the right (longer time) on the CCT diagram.

That's why a plain-carbon steel like 1045 needs a severe water or brine quench to form martensite — and even then, only in thin sections, because the inside of a thick part cools too slowly and the center misses the narrow window. An alloy steel like 4140 or 4340 has its C-curve nose pushed far enough right that a slower, less distortion-prone oil quench (or even air cooling in thin sections) is enough to miss the nose and still form martensite. This is also why alloy steels are chosen for parts that must harden all the way through a thick cross-section.

Hardenability is measured directly with the Jominy end-quench test (ASTM A255): one end of a standard bar is water-quenched while the rest air-cools along its length, producing a range of cooling rates in a single specimen, and hardness is measured along that length. See AISI/SAE steel designation codes for how alloy content relates to grade.

Reading a Cooling Curve

To predict the resulting microstructure for a given cooling curve:

StepWhat to Check
1Follow the cooling curve down from the austenitizing temperature.
2Does it cross the Ps curve? If yes, note the temperature/time of crossing — pearlite formation begins there.
3Does it cross Pf before exiting the pearlite region? If yes, pearlite formation completes; if the curve exits first, only partial pearlite forms and the rest of the austenite is still available to transform further down.
4Repeat for Bs/Bf if the curve reaches the bainite region with untransformed austenite remaining.
5Any austenite still untransformed when the curve crosses Ms converts to martensite instantly; anything remaining below Mf is essentially all martensite (small amounts of retained austenite are common in higher-carbon/alloy steels).
6The room-temperature microstructure is the sum of whatever formed at each stage — e.g., a curve that partially crosses Ps/Pf and then Ms yields a pearlite + martensite mixture.
