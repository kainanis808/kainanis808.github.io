Aluminum Alloy & Temper Designation System

How to read wrought aluminum alloy numbers and temper codes

The Aluminum Association (AA) assigns every wrought aluminum alloy a four-digit number. Unlike steel, where all four digits encode composition, only the first digit reliably tells you something on its own — the alloy's principal alloying element (its "series"). A separate letter-and-number temper code attached after the alloy number (e.g. 6061-T6) describes what was done to the metal after it was made — that code, not the alloy number, is what tells you the strength and hardness you're actually getting.

How to Read a Four-Digit Alloy Number

The first digit identifies the series (major alloying element). The second digit indicates a modification of the original alloy (0 = original composition; 1–9 = a registered variant). For the 1xxx series only, the last two digits give the minimum aluminum purity above 99%. For all other series, the last two digits simply identify the specific alloy within that series — they do not encode a percentage the way steel's carbon digits do.

Example: 1060 — 1xxx series, 99.60% min. aluminum (last 2 digits = purity above 99%)

Wrought Alloy Series (Aluminum Association)

All eight wrought series, the element that defines each one, and whether the series responds to solution heat treatment (T tempers) or only to strain hardening (H tempers).

Series Principal Alloying Element Heat Treatable? Key Characteristics & Typical Uses Common Alloys
1xxx None — 99.00%+ pure aluminum Strain-hardened only Excellent corrosion resistance and electrical conductivity, very low strength. Chemical/food equipment, foil, electrical conductors. 1100, 1050, 1060
2xxx Copper (Cu) Heat treatable High strength approaching steel, but lower corrosion resistance — often clad with pure aluminum ("Alclad"). Aircraft structure, rivets. 2024, 2014, 2219
3xxx Manganese (Mn) Strain-hardened only Moderate strength, good workability and corrosion resistance. Cookware, roofing, HVAC, beverage-can bodies. 3003, 3004, 3105
4xxx Silicon (Si) Mostly not heat treatable Lowers melting point and improves flow — used mainly as filler metal, not structural. Welding/brazing filler wire; anodizes to a gray/black architectural finish. 4043, 4047
5xxx Magnesium (Mg) Strain-hardened only Best corrosion resistance of any structural aluminum series, especially in seawater; good weldability, moderate–high strength. Marine hardware, pressure vessels, auto body panels. 5052, 5083, 5086
6xxx Magnesium + Silicon (Mg₂Si) Heat treatable Good formability, weldability, and corrosion resistance with medium strength — the most common general-purpose extrusion alloy family. Structural shapes, architectural extrusions, frames. 6061, 6063
7xxx Zinc (Zn) Heat treatable Highest-strength aluminum series, but lower corrosion resistance and weldability than 2xxx/6xxx. High-stress aerospace structure. 7075, 7050
8xxx Other elements (Li, Fe, Sn, etc.) Varies by alloy Catch-all series for compositions outside the other seven — includes lithium alloys prized for low density in aerospace, and foil alloys. 8006, 8111

Second digit = registered modification of the original alloy (0 = original). Only 2xxx, 6xxx, 7xxx, and some 4xxx/8xxx alloys are heat treatable by solution treatment and aging (T tempers); the rest gain strength only through cold working (H tempers).

Temper Designation System

The temper suffix (attached after the alloy number with a hyphen, e.g. 6061-T6) describes the mechanical/thermal processing the metal received — this is what actually determines the strength and hardness you'll measure, not the alloy number alone.

Basic Temper Meaning
FAs fabricated — no special control over strain hardening or thermal treatment
OAnnealed — softest, most ductile, fully recrystallized temper (wrought products only)
HStrain hardened — strength increased by cold working, applies to non-heat-treatable series
WSolution heat treated — an unstable temper that applies only to alloys that age spontaneously at room temperature after solution treatment; always shown with the time elapsed (e.g. W½ hr)
TThermally treated to a stable condition, sometimes plus strain hardening — see T1–T10 below

H — Strain-Hardened Subdesignations

The first digit after H tells you how the alloy was hardened; the second digit (1–9) tells you how much.

CodeMeaning
H1xStrain hardened only, no supplementary thermal treatment
H2xStrain hardened, then partially annealed to reduce strength to the desired level
H3xStrain hardened, then stabilized by a low-temperature thermal treatment (mainly Mg-bearing 5xxx alloys, to prevent age-softening)
Hx2 / Hx4 / Hx6 / Hx8Second digit = degree of strain hardening: 2 = quarter hard, 4 = half hard, 6 = three-quarter hard, 8 = full hard
Hx9Extra hard — beyond the standard full-hard (8) temper

T — Thermally Treated Subdesignations

CodeMeaning
T1Cooled from an elevated-temperature forming process, then naturally aged
T2Cooled from hot working, cold worked, then naturally aged
T3Solution heat treated, cold worked, then naturally aged
T4Solution heat treated, then naturally aged
T5Cooled from an elevated-temperature forming process, then artificially aged
T6Solution heat treated, then artificially aged — the most common structural temper (peak strength)
T7Solution heat treated, then overaged/stabilized for dimensional stability rather than peak strength
T8Solution heat treated, cold worked, then artificially aged
T9Solution heat treated, artificially aged, then cold worked
T10Cooled from hot working, cold worked, then artificially aged

An extra digit is sometimes added after T6/T7 (e.g. T651) to indicate a stress-relief step — check a mill cert or spec sheet for what the full code means on a given part.
